# Video structure for Biology lessons

**Council decision 19 September 2026, corrected the same day by Durai.**
Applies to 9700 Biology first; Maths and Chemistry adapt rather than re-deliberating.

## The division of labour — read this first

| Artifact | Job | Interactive? | Gated? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Notes** | teach step by step, measure understanding | **yes** | **yes** |
| **Video** | explain well, visually | no | no |

**Videos are explanations.** Good visualisation of the components, detailed explanation, meaningful
animation. They are passive by design. All interactivity and all gating live on the **notes** side.

The council originally assumed `video -> gate -> next video`, making the video the container. That is
wrong for this product. The **note** is the container and the gated surface; the video is an
explanation asset. This correction resolves the council's one real split: the panel divided 2-2 on
whether a video should cover one checkpoint or one coherent explanation, and the "one checkpoint"
case rested entirely on remediation targeting — knowing which segment to re-teach on failure. With
gating on the notes, remediation happens there, against the note's checkpoints. **So the video is
sized by explanatory coherence, not by checkpoint.**

## Chunking — by coherent explanation

`Section -> subtopic -> explanation -> teaching beats`

An explanation has ONE concrete objective: *"Explain how capillary structure enables rapid exchange"*
— not "Capillaries", not "leaves 17-19".

To build one:
1. Take one observable outcome the student should be able to explain.
2. Pull in the minimum teaching it needs, within the section's calibration and DO-NOT-ADD list.
3. Budget **3-4 substantive beats**, ~35-45s each.
4. Split wherever the explanation exceeds that budget, **at a meaningful conceptual boundary** —
   never mid-causal-chain, and never by accelerating narration.

Many-to-many with the leaves by design: several definition leaves may serve one explanation; a long
process leaf may span several. **Do not make 239 videos because there are 239 leaves.**

| Phase | Time | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Orient | 10-15s | pose the biological question, locate the structure |
| Build | 35-50s | establish the structures / initial state |
| Explain | 40-55s | the mechanism and its causal relationships |
| Express | 35-50s | translate the mechanism into precise language |
| Close | 10-20s | the creditworthy sentence, and one reject card |

**Arithmetic check (corrected 19 Sep after the section 04 pilot).** The original windows summed to a
170s maximum, while 280 words at 145 wpm with 35% silence needs ~178s — the budgets contradicted each
other and the pilot agent caught it. The windows above sum to 190s, so the word budget fits without
compressing narration. If a phase range and the word budget ever conflict again, **keep the pace and
split the explanation** — never speed up the narration to fit.

**Target 2-3 minutes. Hard ceiling 4 minutes — a build check, not a reviewer's taste.** An overlong
explanation is SPLIT or SIMPLIFIED, never sped up.

**Word budget 280-360 words per ~3 minutes at ~145 wpm** — roughly 35% silence, so there is time to
actually look at the diagram. Do NOT treat 154 wpm x runtime as a script quota; 4 minutes at 154 wpm
is 600+ words and guarantees overload.

## Situate the mechanism — every explanation needs a reason to exist (Durai, biochemist, after pilot v2)

The section 04 pilot teaches *how* a signalling molecule binds — complementary shapes — and never says
**why signalling exists at all**. Durai: *"we miss the opportunity to summarise the big picture,
signalling does not happen in a vacuum and there is a reason it happens."*

A student finishes able to answer the exam question, without the thing that makes it make sense.

**The rule: open or close every explanation by naming the biological problem the mechanism solves.**
One or two sentences. For this topic:

> A multicellular organism has a coordination problem. Cells are separate compartments and cannot
> share cytoplasm, so they must signal chemically — but a molecule released into the blood reaches
> every cell. Receptor specificity is what turns that broadcast into an **addressed message**: insulin
> passes every cell and acts only where the receptor exists. That is why complementary shape matters.

### This does NOT conflict with the do-not-add discipline

Two different axes, and conflating them is what stripped the framing out in the first place:

- **DEPTH of content** — governed by the calibration and the DO-NOT-ADD list. Do not pull in later
  machinery, do not exceed the syllabus ceiling. Unchanged.
- **FRAMING** — why the mechanism exists. Adds no new examinable material, no new worked example,
  no extra depth. It is one or two sentences of purpose.

Scope discipline must never strip the reason. A mechanism with no reason is a fact to memorise;
a mechanism with a reason is understanding. The ceiling governs how FAR we go, not whether the
student is told what any of it is FOR.

**Check before shipping:** can a student say, in one sentence, why this exists in a living thing?
If not, the explanation is incomplete regardless of how well the mechanism is drawn.

## Hook them, then give them a handle (Durai)

Durai: *"we have to bring home the point, make it easy to remember ... picture a lock and a key ...
and we want engagement — 'ever wondered how molecules make it through the membrane', 'ever wondered
how hormones...' that kind of thing."*

Two distinct devices doing two different jobs. Every explanation gets both.

### 1. The HOOK — opens the Orient phase, buys attention

A real question a student might actually have wondered, phrased in their language, not the
syllabus's. It must be answered by the end of the video.

| Topic | Hook |
|---|---|
| Cell signalling | *Ever wondered how a hormone finds exactly the right organ — when your blood carries it absolutely everywhere?* |
| Membrane transport | *Ever wondered how sugar gets into a cell, when the membrane is built to keep water-loving things out?* |
| Enzymes | *Ever wondered why a fever above 40 degrees is actually dangerous?* |
| Immunity | *Ever wondered why you can catch a cold twice, but measles only once?* |
| Respiration | *Ever wondered why you keep breathing hard after you have stopped running?* |
| Transport in plants | *Ever wondered how a tree gets water to a leaf thirty metres up, with no pump?* |

Bad hooks: "In this video we will look at cell signalling." That is a table of contents, not a
question. If it does not make someone mildly curious, rewrite it.

### 2. The HANDLE — the thing they carry out and reuse

A concrete image that makes the idea retrievable weeks later. Lock and key for receptors. An
address on an envelope for specificity. A revolving door for a carrier protein. The handle is what
lets a student reconstruct the mechanism when they have forgotten the wording.

### The guard — and this one is not optional

**The analogy makes it memorable. It is NEVER the exam answer.**

Mark schemes credit precise biological relationships and refuse loose paraphrase. A student who
writes *"the molecule is like a key that fits the lock"* has written an analogy, not biology, and
earns nothing.

So every handle must be followed, in the same breath, by the creditworthy sentence:

> Picture a lock and a key. **Written properly: the signalling molecule binds to a specific receptor
> because their shapes are complementary.**

Prefer analogies the subject itself already uses — lock and key is standard for enzymes and
receptors, so it is safe. Invented metaphors ("the membrane is like a bouncer") are riskier: they
stick, and then students write them in exams. If an analogy cannot be paired with a precise sentence,
do not use it.

### Where each lands

- **HOOK** — first line of Orient, before the objectives card.
- **The reason it exists** — Orient or Close (see the rule above on situating the mechanism).
- **HANDLE** — introduced in Build or Explain, then immediately converted into the precise sentence.
- **Express** — the creditworthy sentence, built clause by clause.
- **Close** — the reject card, and a callback answering the hook.

## Pace control: time-stretch, not parameters (tested 19 Sep)

Target is ~145 wpm. Chatterbox clones do not hit it on their own, and **`cfg_weight` does not control
pace** — measured 156 / 153 / 156 wpm at 0.45 / 0.30 / 0.20, which is noise. It changes delivery
character, not speed. Do not tune it for pacing.

**The fix that works, and Durai approved by ear:**

```bash
ffmpeg -i narration.wav -filter:a "atempo=$(echo "145/$MEASURED_WPM" | bc -l)" narration-paced.wav
```

`atempo` preserves pitch. At mild ratios (~0.93) Durai judged it *"sounds best ... better than cfg"*.

**ORDER MATTERS — stretch BEFORE transcription:**

```
generate -> measure actual wpm -> atempo to target -> Whisper -> map cues -> render
```

Cue timestamps must come from the FINAL stretched audio. Transcribing first and stretching after
drifts every visual reveal, and the drift grows through the video.

**Limits.** Keep the ratio above ~0.85; beyond that the stretch becomes audible. If a voice needs
more correction than that, fix the REFERENCE recording instead — a clone imitates its reference, so a
sample delivered at teaching pace produces a narration at teaching pace. Parameters and filters are
for the last few percent, not for undoing a brisk reference.

## BREVITY IS A CHAT HABIT, NOT A TEACHING ONE (Durai, 20 Sep)

Durai: *"I typically want to ask LLMs to be brief and straight to the point here in chat, and here it
seems we are shying away from detail and taking a student into confidence."*

He is identifying a real pull. Assistant default register is compression — say the thing, do not pad.
That is correct in a chat window and **wrong in a lesson**. Combined with a duration cap it produced
exactly the bullet-point delivery he rejected.

**When writing narration, invert the usual instinct.** The failure mode here is not verbosity, it is
terseness. A student who has never held a microscope needs:

- the thing named, then described, then shown
- the reason it is done that way
- what goes wrong if it is not
- and the sentence that connects it to what came before

None of that is padding. All of it would be cut by a reflex to be concise.

**Hand-holding is the goal, not a risk.** Durai: *"this needs to walk the student through, like
holding their hand."* Waffle is still waffle — repetition, filler, restating. But detail that serves
understanding is the job.

## NARRATION REGISTER — talk to the student, do not brief them (Durai, 20 Sep)

Durai on the first Topic 1.1 build: *"It's bullet point and not properly narrated... I don't want to
call it AI-like, but it's too matter-of-fact, straight to the point. I want to tell a story of what
the student would actually go through."*

### Address the student and say what is required of them

**Not:** *"We will look at the microscope. We will draw a cell."* That is a contents list read aloud.

**Instead**, in Durai's own framing:

> In this section we are looking at the structure of a cell. As an A Level student you will be
> required to view cell structure on a light microscope, and there are key things you are required
> to know. You need to be proficient with the microscope itself — this is one thing you cannot get
> away without knowing.

Second person. Say what the exam will ask of them and why it matters, then teach it.

### Construct sentences, tell a story, do not waffle

Durai: *"talk, converse — not bullet point, not matter-of-fact straight to the point. Construct
sentences, make it tell a story without waffling."*

Two failure modes, and the fix is between them:
- **Bullet points read aloud** — each sentence a self-contained fact, no connective tissue. This is
  what the first build did.
- **Waffle** — padding, restating, filling time.

The middle is a person explaining to one student: sentences that carry from one to the next, asides
where something is surprising or easy to get wrong, and a thread running through the whole thing.

## DEMONSTRATING AN ERROR MUST BE UNMISTAKABLY SIGNPOSTED (Durai, 20 Sep)

Durai: *"the part where we demonstrate the errors, we need to be more explicit that now we are
showing the common mistakes."*

Performing a mistake is one of the strongest devices we have — the trapped air bubble, the off-centre
step-up, the drawing that breaks every convention. But an unlabelled error is a teaching hazard: a
student who joins late, looks up mid-beat, or half-watches will absorb the wrong thing as the method.

**Every error demonstration is explicitly framed, three ways at once:**

1. **Said, before it happens.** *"Now let us get it wrong deliberately, because you will meet this."*
   Never begin performing an error without announcing it.
2. **Marked on screen, for the whole beat.** A persistent treatment that cannot be missed or
   mistaken for the method — a distinct label such as **COMMON MISTAKE**, a tinted border or desaturated
   frame around the error state, terracotta rather than the normal accent. It must be visible in any
   single frame taken from that beat, because someone will see exactly one frame.
3. **Closed with the correction, in the same beat.** Never end on the error. Perform the fix, and
   return to the correct state before moving on.

**The existing hard rule still applies:** show the error WRITTEN, speak only the correct version.
Do not put a wrong phrasing in the student's ear.

### The error beat has FIVE moves — talk the mistake through (Durai, 21 Sep, after watching Topic 1)

*"We display it on the screen, take a pause and then describe it… I think we need to describe the
mistake better whilst it's on the screen. The pause is good, gives a chance for the students to
look at it — but immediately after, add an additional pause to quickly talk through the mistake and
highlight it using Remotion to show the issues."*

In the twelve Topic 1 lessons the card appeared, held in silence, and the narration then went
straight to the CORRECT answer. The student saw a wrong answer and heard a right one, and nobody
ever told them what was wrong with the first or why people write it. From Topic 2 on, every error
beat runs in this order:

1. **Announce** — "Here is a mistake examiners see a lot." The COMMON MISTAKE marker comes on.
2. **Show** — the wrong answer appears, written, exactly as a student would write it.
3. **Silent read** — 3–4 s, no narration. The student reads it and tries to spot the problem.
4. **TALK IT THROUGH — new.** The card STAYS on screen and the marker stays on. The narration walks
   through the mistake while the animation points at it: the offending word or number is ringed,
   underlined or pulsed AT THE MOMENT it is discussed (cue-synced like any other visual); an arrow
   or side-note shows what it was confused with; if two ideas were merged, they are pulled apart on
   screen. Say three things: **where** the error is, **why** students make it, and **why it loses
   the mark** — quote the examiner report or mark scheme if there is one. **Measured on the first
   twelve Topic 2 storyboards: a talk-through written properly costs 40–55 seconds**, not the
   10–20 first guessed. Keep it tight, but it is what it costs.
5. **Correct** — the marker clears, the wrong words are struck or replaced IN PLACE so the student
   watches the wrong answer become the right one, and the narration gives the creditworthy version.
   **When the wrong answer has several faults** (a ring drawn with three wrong carbons), the marker
   stays on until the LAST one is corrected and clears only on the completed correct frame —
   clearing after the first fix leaves wrong content on screen unmarked (found by the check gate on
   2.2.1, 21 Sep).

**The badge must tell the truth about the evidence (conductor's ruling, 22 Sep — found on the built
2.2.10).** The marker reads **COMMON MISTAKE** only when an examiner report, or a mark-scheme reject
line, shows this is an error candidates actually make. When the beat is built on a real wrong
OPTION or a real question with no report saying how often or why candidates chose it — an
assessment-evidenced contrast, with our own interpretation of the reasoning — the marker reads
**EXAM CONTRAST**, same colour, same five moves, same hold-until-corrected timing. A frame that says
COMMON MISTAKE above a caption saying "not a diagnosed common error" contradicts itself, and the
badge is what the student remembers. `MistakeBeat` takes the label as a prop; the default stays
COMMON MISTAKE. The checker confirms the label against the evidence for every error beat.

**How this lives with "written, never spoken".** That rule exists so a wrong claim never lands in
the student's ear as a fact — and it still holds. The talk-through POINTS AT the mistake; it does
not assert it. Not: *"A bacterium has a cellulose cell wall."* But: *"Look at the wall material in
this answer. That is the plant's — it is the word students reach for because 'cell wall' makes them
think of plants. A bacterium's wall is peptidoglycan, and the mark scheme rejects anything else."*
The wrong word may be named as a word under discussion ("the word 'cellulose' here"), never spoken
as a statement about biology. If the only way to explain an error would be to read the wrong
sentence aloud as a claim, rewrite the explanation.

**For storyboards and checks:** the talk-through is narration, so it is written out in full, counted
in the runtime, cue-mapped to its highlights, and checked like every other line — including the
absolutes sweep ("students always…" is itself an over-claim; "a common slip is…" is not). **Budget
~45 seconds per evidenced error beat** (weights written before 21 Sep allowed 15 s — add 30 s per
error beat to them). Never cut an evidenced error beat to reach a time budget; cut repetition first.

**Name it as common, and say why it happens.** "Students draw this and label it as a nucleus" is
more useful than "this is wrong" — it tells them it is a normal trap rather than personal stupidity,
which is what makes them willing to check for it.

### Do not let the ERRORS become a bullet list either

Durai, on the same beat: *"'the other mistake students make is...', 'also watch...' — I thought
going through as bullets there was a little off too."*

We fixed the narration register and then wrote the error section as an enumeration of errors. *Fault
one, fault two, fault three* is still bullets; it is just bullets about mistakes.

**Weave them.** The register is a teacher who has marked a lot of papers telling you what they keep
seeing:

> *"The other mistake students make is leaving a fold in the peel — and you can spot it, because one
> set of walls comes sharp and a second set behind it never will. Also watch your light. If the whole
> field looks washed out and ghostly, that is not your specimen, that is the iris wide open."*

Not:

> *"Mistake 2: folded peel. Mistake 3: iris too open."*

Connective phrases carry from one to the next — *the other mistake*, *also watch*, *and the one
people miss*, *while we are here*. Each error still gets its own signposted visual beat; it is the
narration that must flow rather than enumerate.

**This applies wherever we list anything** — faults, conventions, apparatus, structures. A list on
screen is fine and often clearer. A list in the ear is the thing Durai keeps rejecting.

## LENGTH VARIES BY CONTENT TYPE — practical is the outlier, not the new normal

Durai, after seeing the 1.1 weights: *"I hear you on the length, I think that is the product and
maybe the competitive advantage. I just found also this topic needs that extra attention being a
practical guide. Going forward I sense that these chapters are broken down sufficiently enough for us
to be able to have shorter videos."*

Removing the cap must not become an assumption that everything runs long. The syllabus granularity
already sorts this, and the Topic 1 weights show the spread:

| Content type | Example | Runs |
|---|---|---|
| **Practical / apparatus** | 1.1.1 preparation, 1.1.4 calibration | **LONG** — 11-12 min. Equipment named and located, handling shown, errors performed and recovered from. |
| **Process or mechanism** | membrane transport, cardiac cycle | medium — the mechanism animates, and that takes the time it takes |
| **Bounded list with a ceiling** | 1.2.1, fifteen named structures | splits naturally into several SHORT videos |
| **Single statement** | 1.2.4 "state that cells use ATP" | **40 seconds.** Do not pad it to look substantial. |

**Length is decided by the weights, per outcome, before the build.** Neither a cap nor a floor.
A video that runs long because the content demands it is the product; a video that runs long because
we removed a limit is waste.

## LONGER NARRATION MEANS MORE VISUAL BEATS, NOT LONGER HOLDS (Durai, 20 Sep)

The necessary corollary to removing the duration cap. Durai: *"with more narration, we keep the video
the same, then we just have sections that are static. Let's not be economical with the visuals and
Remotion — that's a real power here, especially for the practical."*

**A longer video must be a RICHER video, not a slower one.** If narration doubles and the visuals do
not, the result is a static frame with a voice over it — worse than the short version, because now it
is also boring.

- Every ~10-15 seconds of narration wants its own visual state. Something appears, moves, is labelled,
  is ringed, changes.
- **A static hold is only legitimate when the student is being asked to LOOK at something specific**
  and has been told what to look for. Otherwise it is dead screen.
- When a script grows, the storyboard grows with it. Count beats against runtime and if the ratio
  drops, the visuals are under-built, not the narration over-written.

**This applies everywhere, but practical content most of all.** Naming apparatus, showing handling,
demonstrating what goes wrong — each of those is a visual beat that a paper resource cannot have.
Durai: *"that's also a way of doing what we are doing always, just more so here."*

Remotion is the asset. Under-using it is the waste.

## DURATION IS CONTENT-LED — the old cap was wrong (corrected 20 Sep)

The previous target of 3:00-3:30 with a 4:00 ceiling was **measured from a MATHS revision provider**
and should never have become a rule for Biology practical work. Durai: *"I don't know if there's
anything in our prompting that is pushing us to that three minute mark. I think we need to do
justice and show detail enough."*

**There is no duration cap.** A topic takes the time it needs.

- Practical and apparatus work runs LONGER — there is physical detail to show and name.
- Splitting is a judgement about coherence, never about a clock.
- The test is not "is it under four minutes" but **"has every outcome been done justice?"**
- Still never pad, and never speed up narration.

**Weight per outcome is planned BEFORE the build** (see the topic plan), so a grouping cannot starve
an outcome. Four outcomes in 3:45 gives each about 55 seconds, which is the bullet-point failure
arriving by arithmetic.

## SHOW AND NAME THE APPARATUS, AND WHERE EACH PART SITS

Durai could not follow the graticule sequence: *"where does the other one sit? where would you slide
the other one? what do you need to be careful about?"*

For any practical outcome:

1. **Name every piece** as it appears — *this is the slide, this is the coverslip, this is the stage
   micrometer*. Label it on screen. A student who has never held one cannot infer it.
2. **Establish WHERE each part physically sits** before using it. The eyepiece graticule sits inside
   the eyepiece; the stage micrometer sits on the stage. Those are different places and the whole
   calibration depends on knowing that. Show the microscope and point to both.
3. **Show the handling** — scraping the sample, which tools, which typical specimens (onion
   epidermis), wiping the slide and coverslip, lowering the coverslip at an angle.
4. **Say what to be careful about** at each step, and what goes wrong if you are not.

Assume no bench experience. That is the student we are serving.

### Handling must be physically possible (Durai, 22 Sep, after watching 2.1.1)

A student copies what they see, so a motion that could not happen on a real bench teaches the wrong
hands. The 2.1.1 build tilted a test tube to 62 degrees and drew the stream leaving the SIDE of the
tube; a bottle "poured" at 70 degrees with no stream at all. Durai: *"this pouring action is not
aligned."*

- **A pour needs the mouth BELOW the base.** A tube or bottle tilted less than 90 degrees still has its
  mouth uppermost and cannot pour. Pouring angles are roughly 110 to 130 degrees from upright.
- **The stream leaves the lip**, and its start point is computed from the vessel's rotated mouth, never
  hardcoded. It lands inside the receiving vessel's mouth, not on its rim or its side.
- **Liquid stays level.** The surface inside a tilted vessel is horizontal and gathers towards the
  lower end, and the level falls as the receiving level rises.
- The same test applies to every handled object: a dropper is squeezed above the tube and never
  touches it, a bung is seated before shaking, a tube is held by a holder when heated.
- **Check it on a still frame.** Pull the mid-pour frame and LOOK before rendering the beat.

### A colour change passes only through colours the reaction really shows (found by the 2.1.1 builder, 22 Sep)

Cross-fading a swatch in RGB invents intermediate colours. Blue fading to brick red passes through
PURPLE, and purple is the positive result of the BIURET test, so a student watching the Benedict's
test saw another test's result mid-heat; a tube in a water bath turned grey. The builder caught this
at its own pre-review.

- A colour result either **switches in one frame** at the moment the narration says the change is
  seen, or animates **only through the real sequence** (Benedict's: blue, green, yellow, orange, brick
  red — and only as far as that sample's concentration takes it).
- Never tween between two reagent colours in RGB. It is the colour analogue of the covalent-bond rule:
  every frame is a state the reaction could really be in.

## THE FULL SHAPE (Durai, 20 Sep — supersedes the phase table where they differ)

Five moves. The first and last are the ones we were getting wrong.

### 1. SET THE CONTEXT — **not a one-liner**

Durai: *"Clear introductory of what it is we are talking about, let's not make it a one-liner ...
the blood flows all over the body, if I wonder what goes where ... just a little bit of talk there
so that the context is solid. Obviously not too long, but the right balance."*

The hook is a question; the context is the **conversation around it**. Give the student the world the
problem lives in before naming the problem. For signalling:

> Your blood goes everywhere. Every cell in your body, all the time. So when your brain decides you
> need to be ready to run, or your pancreas notices sugar in your blood, whatever it releases is
> going to reach *everything* — your eyes, your toes, your liver, all of it. And yet only some of
> those cells do anything about it. Something has to be deciding what goes where.

**~20-35 seconds.** Long enough that the context is solid, short enough that it is still an opening.
A single line of "ever wondered..." is too thin — it poses the question without making the student
feel the problem.

### 2. WHAT YOU WILL LEARN — plain bullets

Unchanged: deliberately plain display, two or three points, no diagram, no animation.

### 3. EXPLAIN IT — scientifically correct AND humanly

Durai: *"cover the scientific part of it and what we will examine, obviously. But it's also good to
really explain it in a human way, and in a way that recognizes that we are speaking to 17 to 19 year
olds ... just a little bit more talk there."*

**Register: talking to a 17-19 year old, not at them.** Two failure modes, both real:
- *Too dry* — a syllabus statement read aloud. Correct and forgettable.
- *Too childish* — they are doing A Levels. Do not explain like they are twelve.

Talking to them sounds like: saying why a thing is surprising, admitting when something is fiddly,
using an aside to make a point land. The handle (lock and key) lives here, converted immediately into
the creditworthy sentence. **Allow the extra talk** — this is where the "little bit more" belongs.

### 4. WHAT I TOLD YOU — recap in place on the familiar visual

Unchanged, and this is the take-home image. Static diagram, key points faded in on structures they
now recognise. Durai: this recap is *"more direct to what we are actually teaching and what comes in
an exam."*

### 5. **NEW — WHAT YOU GET ASKED IN THE EXAM**

Durai: *"is there a place where you would actually address what typically comes in an exam or what
you get questioned on ... typical exam questions, just as a summary."*

A short closing beat naming the forms the examiner actually uses. **Not practice questions** — those
live in the notes and practice surface. This is orientation: *this is what it looks like when they
ask you.*

**We already have the evidence for this and are not using it.** The gate-criteria files carry
measured frequencies and the cited exclusions:

- Pure: *inverse functions appear in 15/15 papers, quadratic solving 15/15, discriminants 14/15.*
- Biology: per-section incidence across the sampled papers, plus every ACCEPT/REJECT wording.

So this beat is **evidence-based, never invented**. For signalling it would be: *they ask you to
explain why a molecule affects one cell and not another. They want "complementary shape" and they
want the causal link. They will not credit "active site" — that belongs to enzymes.*

**~15-25 seconds.** Ends on the reject card.

### On length

This adds perhaps 40 seconds over the current 2:45. **Accept it.** Durai's instruction is "the right
balance", not "keep it short" — a video that is tight but leaves a student without context or exam
orientation has optimised the wrong thing. Target **3:00-3:30**; split the explanation if it runs past
4:00, and never speed the narration up to fit.

## THE GOVERNING STRUCTURE — tell them, tell them, tell them (Durai)

Durai, who presented this way for years: *"I start by early on after the hook telling you what I am
going to tell you. Then I tell you — including why it's important. Then I tell you what I told you."*

This is the spine of every explanation. The phases below serve it; where anything in this document
conflicts with it, this wins.

### 1. HOOK
A real question the student might have wondered. See the hook rules above.

### 2. LEARNING OBJECTIVES — "what I am going to tell you"

**CORRECTED 20 Sep — I had this wrong.** Durai: *"my earlier prompt on the video to say it's basic
was wrong... what I wanted to say is that the recap can be illustrated on diagrams but the initial
does not get illustrated on diagrams. I didn't mean that the delivery must be poor or poorly
presented or boring — let's make it engaging."*

The distinction is **ANCHORING, not production value**:

- **Objectives are NOT illustrated on the lesson diagram** — the student has not met it yet, so
  putting objectives on a diagram they cannot read teaches nothing.
- **The recap IS on that diagram**, because by then it is familiar. See move 4.

**So: style the objectives properly.** Typography, motion, brand. Make them worth watching. This is
the first thing a student sees and it sets whether they stay. It simply lives in its own visual
world rather than on the diagram.

Lead with them — Durai: *"we lead with the learning objectives."* Two or three, in the student's
language, stating what they will be able to do.

The objectives card should be visually strong in its own right. What it must NOT do is sit on the
lesson diagram, because the student has not learned that diagram yet.

### 3. EXPLAIN — "telling you, including why it's important"
**Here is where all the visual work goes.** The diagram builds, the mechanism animates, the handle
lands and converts into the precise sentence.

**"Including why it's important" is not optional** — this is the bigger picture, the small talk that
brings it home. The biological problem the mechanism solves. See the rule on situating the mechanism.

### 4. RECAP — "what I told you"
The part most explainers get wrong, and the most valuable.

**Do NOT make a new summary card.** Return to the SAME visual the student has just spent two minutes
learning. It is now familiar — that familiarity is the whole asset, and a fresh slide throws it away.

The diagram can go static. Then **fade in or highlight the key points IN PLACE**, on the structures
they now recognise:

- the receptor they watched the molecule fail to seat into, now labelled with the point about specificity
- the bilayer they saw built, now carrying the point about what can and cannot cross

Durai: *"highlighting or fading in to show the keypoints on the visual now that the students are
familiar with and that they will take home with."*

The image becomes the memory anchor. A student recalling this topic in an exam should see that
diagram with those points on it.

### Why this ordering works

The preview tells them what to listen for, so the explanation lands on prepared ground. The recap
attaches the points to an image they already hold, so it is retrievable. Three passes at the same
idea, each doing a different job — and only the middle one needs to be beautiful.

### Mapping to the production phases

| Rhetorical move | Phase | Visual treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | Orient | minimal — a question on screen |
| **Learning objectives** | Orient | **styled and engaging, but NOT on the lesson diagram** |
| Explain (+ why it matters) | Build / Explain | full visual craft, animated mechanism |
| Precise sentence | Express | clause by clause, diagram held |
| **Recap** | Close | **the SAME diagram, static, key points highlighted in place** |
| Reject card | Close | cited wrong/right pair |

## Silence must be anchored (Durai, after the section 04 pilot)

The pilot held ~36% silence as "looking time". On screen those holds **read as unanswered questions**
— the student senses they are being asked something, is not told what, and can get lost.

**A pause must always have an on-screen anchor.** Two devices:

1. **An explicit "what you should be able to explain" card**, at the start and wherever a hold lands.
   Frame it as the thing to speak to, not as a mystery:
   > *By the end you should be able to explain: why a signalling molecule binds one particular receptor.*
   Put it on a **distinct background colour** so it is unmistakably a different KIND of moment from
   the explanation. Use motion to bring it in — this is a signpost, and it should feel like one.
2. **Then go into the explanation.** The card sets the target; the explanation delivers it.

Never leave a silent hold over a static diagram with nothing stated. Either the student is looking at
something changing, or they are being told what to look for.

## Animate the MECHANISM, not the states (Durai, after the section 04 pilot)

The pilot showed the non-matching molecule sitting NEAR the receptor and said it does not bind. That
is a picture of a fact. **Show the attempt and the failure**: move the wrong-shaped molecule DOWN
onto the binding site, let it visibly fail to seat, then move it away. Then bring the matching one
down and let it seat cleanly. The contrast is then something the student SEES happen, not something
they are told.

Durai: *"just make proper use of Remotion because we want our visualizations to be impactful and to
have meaning."*

- A state change the narration asserts should be a **movement on screen**, not a cut between stills.
- Binding, transport, diffusion, a gradient, a conformational change — all of these are motions.
  If the narration says something happens, the diagram should perform it.
- This does NOT license decoration. The test stays: does the movement carry the explanation? Tracing
  an outline for effect still fails. A molecule failing to seat passes.

### When covalent bonds change: every frame must be valid chemistry (check gate, Topic 2, 21 Sep)

"Animate the mechanism" is about MOTION — approach, fit, failure to seat. It does not license
inventing a reaction mechanism. A condensation or hydrolysis drawn as "the OH drifts off, the H
drifts off, then the link forms" puts free fragments, a five-bonded carbon or a two-bonded hydrogen
on screen for half a second, and a paused frame is a wrong diagram. It took four check rounds to get
this out of Topic 2; the rule now is:

- A reaction that makes or breaks covalent bonds is shown as a **net transformation**: two complete
  keyframes, and the COMPLETE bond graph switches in **one rendered frame**. No cross-fade — a fade
  shows old and new bonds together. Whole molecules and labelled bookkeeping copies may move smoothly
  before and after; bonds do not.
- The caption **"net reaction; not a stepwise mechanism"** is visible throughout, on every replay.
- The storyboard lists the edges: bonds DELETED and bonds CREATED, by atom name. **Water counts.**
  Hydrolysis deletes a water O–H as it creates the two product bonds; condensation creates one. An
  edge list that forgets the water balances at the endpoints and fails in between.
- The builder runs a **valence audit** on every rendered state (C 4, N 3, O 2, H 1), not just on the
  keyframes. The checker does the same count by hand on the storyboard's edge list.
- Narration may do atom bookkeeping ("from the carboxyl group, the O H leaves… those three atoms
  become one molecule of water") while the reactants stay bonded on screen. It must not describe
  intermediates the syllabus does not teach.
- Non-covalent events — hydrogen bonds forming, a substrate entering an active site, a protein
  unfolding — ARE motions and should be animated as such.

## Visual rule — one line, absolute

> **If a mark scheme could credit it, it is authored vector.**

| Tier | Use | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Structural SVG | the bulk of screen time | progressively revealed by `useCue`; labels are **SVG text nodes** from a per-section `TERMS.md`, **never pixels** |
| Pre-drawn ink | annotation only | arrows, the ring on the spoken figure (TEACHING-STANDARD §14). Ink POINTS AT structure, never IS structure. Never drawn at runtime. |
| Generated image | context only | <=1 per video, first 20s, no labels, no arrows. **OCR it — any legible text means reject and regenerate.** |

**Micrographs are SOURCED, never generated.** Section 1 examines micrograph interpretation; a
hallucinated micrograph is the worst asset we could ship.

**Meaningful animation, not theatrical drawing.** Establish the spatial scaffold fast, then reveal as
explained. Movement should express something real — transport, sequence, binding, change of state.
Do not slowly trace every membrane outline for effect; it costs seconds and teaches nothing.
Keep orientation and colour meaning stable across a section.

**~25 canonical diagrams as a section-level shared library** — the membrane serves sections 1, 4, 11
and 14, and the same assets are reused by the notes, the questions and the corrections. That shared
library is the expensive part and the reason the whole plan is affordable.

**QA the RENDERED SEQUENCE, not the source art** — a contact sheet, one frame per cue, reviewed
against the gate criteria and DO-NOT-ADD list. A correct diagram goes wrong through animation timing.

## Teaching wording precision

Maths develops a line of working; **Biology develops a sentence.** Build it clause by clause as the
diagram develops alongside:

> **AGENT · ACTION · TARGET · DIRECTION/CONDITION · CAUSAL LINK**

> *The capillary wall is one endothelial cell thick, **so** the diffusion distance is short.*

Highlight the named structure, the property and the causal connective. Close every video with **one
reject card**: `✗ the bacteria become immune` / `✓ the resistant bacteria survive and reproduce`,
struck through by hand, citation in small type.

Two hard rules:
1. **Show the error written; speak only the correct one.** Never plant a rejected phrase in the ear.
2. **The narration is itself a model answer.** Lint every script against the section's REJECT list
   **before TTS** — and lint the PRE-NORMALISATION script, because the normalizer rewrites words and
   a rewritten word must never be a cue keyword.

Model correct language first, introduce at most one relevant contrast, then return to the correct
form. **Do not turn a lesson into a list of forbidden words** — the REJECT rows are question-local,
not a global ban.

## SAY THE TYPICAL THING AS TYPICAL (21 Sep — from the 1.2 check gate)

Seven 1.2 storyboards went through an independent check before any narration was bought. Six came
back REWORK, and one defect outnumbered all the others: **a statement that is true locally, spoken
as if it were true universally.** Every instance read well. Every instance was wrong.

| What the draft said | What is actually true |
|---|---|
| A bacterium has "no compartments" | The syllabus says no organelles surrounded by **double** membranes. The June 2023 examiner report refuses single-membrane organelles as a eukaryote discriminator — "known to be present in prokaryotes". |
| A eukaryotic cell's DNA is inside the nucleus | The **main** DNA is. Mitochondria and chloroplasts carry their own — which the next lesson depends on. |
| "A vacuole never counts as evidence of a plant cell" | ONE mark scheme ignored it for ONE pictured tissue, and credited wall, regular shape, no cleavage furrow, cell plate. |
| "Every human cell has lysosomes" | Mature red blood cells have none. |
| The nucleolus makes ribosomes — "nothing else makes them" | Over-claim added for rhythm. |
| "Every micrograph records one thin slice" | True of a TEM section. An SEM records a surface; a light micrograph can be a whole mount. |
| Eukaryotes have compartments BECAUSE they are too big for one open bag | A just-so story. The mark schemes credit the FUNCTION ("compartmentalisation … provides optimum conditions"), never an evolutionary cause. |

**The rule.** Before a storyboard leaves its author, every sentence containing *every, all, always,
never, only, no, nothing else, cannot, because* is re-read with one question: **is this true of all
cases, or only of the typical case I have in mind?** Say the typical thing as typical — "a typical
bacterium", "the main DNA", "in this image" — which is also, not by accident, how the syllabus and
the mark schemes phrase it.

**Why it happens.** The reference storyboard (1.1.1) works by giving ONE causal idea and making
everything a consequence. For the microscope that idea was physics, and physics is universal. Cell
biology is a science of typical cases and exceptions; reach for a universal cause there and you
invent one. A spine for a biology lesson must be defensible from the syllabus wording and from what
mark schemes CREDIT — quote the credited words in the storyboard.

**Rework breeds it.** In every check round, fixing one over-claim introduced another — the rewrite
is where the author reaches for a clean sentence. So: keep the change surface small, never "improve"
a cleared sentence, and re-check after every rework. A citation is quoted exactly or not at all:
"fewer candidates recognised…" is not "most candidates got it wrong".

**The gate itself is now standard**: author → independent cross-engine check against syllabus,
papers and this document → rework → re-check, until the checker would stake the audio budget on
the text. Only then is narration bought. The narration is then FROZEN — a builder who cannot build
a line as written stops and reports.
