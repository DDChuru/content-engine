## Outcomes in syllabus order

### 4.1.1

**Syllabus verbatim — [p21](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=21):**

> describe the fluid mosaic model of membrane structure with reference to the hydrophobic and hydrophilic interactions that account for the formation of the phospholipid bilayer and the arrangement of proteins

**Commands / content type:** describe. **STRUCTURE / MODEL**. Budget **5:00** of the 9:00 lesson 4.1.1-2, 0 errors.

**What we teach.** A phospholipid (recalled by label from Topic 2: glycerol, two fatty-acid tails, a phosphate-containing head) has a **hydrophilic head** and **hydrophobic tails**. In water, phospholipids arrange themselves so the heads face the water on both sides and the tails face each other, away from the water: a **bilayer**. Taught as interactions, not as the tails "hating" water: the heads interact with water (hydrophilic interactions, including hydrogen bonds from water to the charged/polar head), while water molecules hydrogen-bond with each other and the non-polar tails are excluded and held together by hydrophobic interactions, so the bilayer is the arrangement in which each part is where it interacts best. Proteins sit in the bilayer according to the same logic: **intrinsic proteins are embedded in the bilayer; the channel, carrier and receptor examples drawn here span it and are transmembrane proteins**, with regions of hydrophobic R groups in contact with the tails and hydrophilic regions in contact with water on each side; **extrinsic (peripheral) proteins** sit on a surface, attached to the heads or to intrinsic proteins. "**Fluid**": phospholipids and many proteins move sideways within their layer; "**mosaic**": proteins scattered through the bilayer like tiles in a mosaic, in varied sizes and positions. Framing (why it exists): a cell is a watery compartment in watery surroundings, and the bilayer is the arrangement that keeps a water-based inside separate from a water-based outside while letting the cell control what crosses; "a model, introduced in 1972, that continues to be modified" (syllabus introduction) is said once.

**Real-world samples.** None handled. An explain-beat example: the membrane drawn is the cell surface membrane of a named cell (a red blood cell), so "outside" is plasma and "inside" is cytoplasm; both labelled.

**Exact mark-scheme keys:** no directly relevant cited part for 4.1.1 (0/5). The lesson's exam close is M24/22 Q1(a)(ii), QP p.3 / MS p.5 (PDF-CHECKED, plan check 006), on cholesterol orientation through polar/non-polar interactions, 1 mark; it validates the 4.1.2 portion and is said not to be a full fluid-mosaic-model question. The describe form for 4.1.1 is taken from the syllabus command and labelled *syllabus-based*.

**Scope ceiling and boundaries:** no membrane thickness figures, no freeze-fracture evidence, no lipid rafts, no flip-flop, no membrane potentials. Roles of the components are 4.1.3's.

**Motion:** self-assembly of scattered phospholipids into a bilayer (and not a micelle: the micelle is not taught) as motion in a water field; lateral drift of phospholipids and proteins; proteins never cross between layers.

**Visual needs:** `FluidMosaicMembrane` (published here), `PhospholipidToken` (published here; Topic 2 recall by label), `WaterField`.

**Recorded examiner evidence / supported error treatment:** none selected; the absence of an allocated beat is not an absence of evidence.

### 4.1.3

**Syllabus verbatim — [p21](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=21):**

> describe the roles of phospholipids, cholesterol, glycolipids, proteins and glycoproteins in cell surface membranes, with reference to stability, fluidity, permeability, transport (carrier proteins and channel proteins), cell signalling (cell surface receptors) and cell recognition (cell surface antigens – see 11.1.2)

**Commands / content type:** describe. **FUNCTION / ROLES**. Budget **10:15** (9:00 teaching + 1:15 complete error beat), 12 teaching beats, **1 error (E43)**.

**What we teach.** Each component's role, on the familiar 4.1.1-2 membrane, organised by the six named headings so no heading is starved:

- *Permeability:* the **phospholipid bilayer's hydrophobic core** is a barrier to ions and to polar (water-soluble) molecules; small non-polar molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide cross it readily. This makes the membrane **partially permeable**. Ions and polar molecules such as glucose do not cross the hydrophobic core readily, so they need a transport protein; in the glucose example drawn, a carrier moves glucose down its concentration gradient. The protein requirement alone does not establish the direction or energy requirement of every glucose-transport system.
- *Transport:* **channel proteins** provide a water-filled, hydrophilic pore through which particular ions or polar molecules pass; **carrier proteins** bind a particular molecule or ion and change shape to move it across. (How each works as a process, passive or active, is 4.2.1a/4.2.1b; here only their role.)
- *Fluidity and stability:* phospholipids moving sideways give fluidity; **cholesterol** regulates fluidity: in the typical account it reduces fluidity at higher temperatures and prevents the tails packing too tightly at low temperatures, and it helps make the membrane more stable and less permeable to small polar molecules and ions. Said with "helps" and "in animal cell membranes"; no temperature numbers. Glycolipids and glycoproteins contribute to stability by forming hydrogen bonds with surrounding water (said as "can help"). Channels are not all alike: each passes particular ions or molecules (no claim that every channel has the same pore selectivity).
- *Cell signalling:* some glycoproteins (and proteins) act as **cell surface receptors** (not every glycoprotein is a receptor): a binding site with a shape complementary to a particular signalling molecule; binding leads to a response inside the cell (the stages are 4.1.4's).
- *Cell recognition:* some glycoproteins and glycolipids act as **cell surface antigens**, so cells can be recognised (self / non-self is 11.1.2's; named, not taught). Not every carbohydrate chain is an ABO antigen. The ABO blood-group example is retained **only with a verified content source at storyboard stage**; without one it is omitted and the recognition role is taught on the generic antigen (`UNVERIFIED — a verified content source for the ABO example`).

Framing: the membrane is not just a wall; each component gives the cell a way to control what crosses, to stay intact, and to communicate.

**Real-world samples.** None handled. Explain-beat examples: oxygen crossing a red blood cell's bilayer; glucose needing a protein route; the ABO example only if sourced (above).

### 4.2.1

**Syllabus verbatim — [p21](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=21):**

> describe and explain the processes of simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis, active transport, endocytosis and exocytosis

**Commands / content type:** describe and explain. **MECHANISM / PROCESS**. Budget **22:00** (18:15 teaching + 3 × 1:15 complete error beats), 24 teaching beats, **3 errors (E45–E47)**. **Delivered as two linked lessons, split at the passive/energy-requiring boundary: 4.2.1a (simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis; 10:45 = 9:30 + 1:15, 13 teaching beats, E45) and 4.2.1b (active transport, endocytosis, exocytosis, and the comparison of facilitated diffusion with active transport; 11:15 = 8:45 + 2:30, 11 teaching beats, E46 and E47).** 4.2.1a ends on its own recap and exam close; 4.2.1b opens on a labelled recall of 4.2.1a's passive processes and the `FluidMosaicMembrane`, then stands alone.

**What we teach — 4.2.1a (passive).** One explanation grammar: particles move randomly because of their kinetic energy; where there is a **concentration gradient**, more particles move from the higher to the lower concentration than the reverse, so there is a **net movement** down the gradient until concentrations are equal (then movement continues both ways, with no net movement). Passive: no energy from ATP is used; the kinetic energy of the particles drives it.

- *Simple diffusion* across a membrane: small, non-polar molecules (O₂, CO₂) pass directly through the phospholipid bilayer, down their concentration gradient. Real-world explain example: oxygen diffusing from alveolar air into a red blood cell (Topic 9 context named, not taught).
- *Facilitated diffusion*: ions and polar molecules do not cross the hydrophobic core readily, so they diffuse through **channel proteins** (a hydrophilic pore) or are moved by **carrier proteins** (binding and a change of shape), still **down** their concentration gradient, still passive. Glucose is polar and does not cross the hydrophobic bilayer core readily; it needs a transport protein. In our facilitated-diffusion example, a carrier moves glucose down its concentration gradient without ATP. The protein requirement alone does not establish the direction or energy requirement of every glucose-transport system. Channel and carrier are kept as two mechanisms; a channel is never given a carrier's shape-change mechanism (G04's own check).
- *Osmosis*: stated in water-potential terms from the start: **osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential, through a partially permeable membrane**. Water potential describes water's tendency to move. Pure water at atmospheric pressure is the reference, with water potential 0 kPa. At the same temperature and pressure, adding solute lowers water potential, so the solutions in this comparison have negative values; a less negative value is higher. Net osmosis is from higher to lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane. Water still crosses both ways when the water potentials are equal. The phrase "concentration of water" is not used (trap table). Water molecules cross in both directions; the net movement is towards the lower water potential. Water crosses the bilayer and, in many cells, channel proteins (aquaporins: named as "in many cells", not taught).
- *Factors on the rate of diffusion*, qualitatively: steeper gradient, larger surface area, shorter distance, higher temperature (more kinetic energy) → faster; small and non-polar molecules cross the bilayer faster. No equation. The surface-area and distance points are handed forward to 4.2.3-4 by label.

Framing: cells must exchange substances with their surroundings all the time, and passive processes cost the cell no ATP; the membrane decides which substances can take which route. Hook (VIDEO-STRUCTURE's own): *Ever wondered how sugar gets into a cell, when the membrane is built to keep water-loving things out?*, answered by facilitated diffusion. Handle: *a door in a wall* for a channel protein, converted at once into *a channel protein provides a hydrophilic pore through which particular ions or polar molecules diffuse down their concentration gradient* (the "revolving door" for a carrier, VIDEO-STRUCTURE's own example, belongs to 4.2.1b's carrier states; each lesson uses only its own handle).

**What we teach — 4.2.1b (energy-requiring and bulk).**

- *Active transport*: movement of molecules or ions across a membrane **against** their concentration gradient (from lower to higher concentration), through **carrier proteins**, using **energy from ATP** made in respiration. Mechanism as motion: the ion binds the carrier's binding site on one side; ATP is hydrolysed (token switch, caption above); the carrier changes shape; the ion is released on the other side; the carrier returns to its original shape. Model guard: the ATP-hydrolysis animation is a simplified directly ATP-driven carrier model. Keep its transported ion generic. Root mineral uptake illustrates energy-dependent accumulation, but do not label the animated ATP-hydrolysing carrier as a nitrate transporter; the example does not specify that molecular mechanism. Co-transport is not taught here. Real-world explain example (separate from the animation): root hair cells accumulating mineral ions such as nitrate from soil water, where the concentration is lower than inside the cell, using energy from respiration (Topic 7 context named).
- *Endocytosis*: the cell surface membrane surrounds material and pinches off to form a **vesicle** inside the cell, using energy from ATP. **Phagocytosis**: solid material, such as a bacterium engulfed by a phagocyte (named; immunity is Topic 11's). **Pinocytosis**: liquid, with dissolved substances, taken in small vesicles.
- *Exocytosis*: a vesicle moves to the cell surface membrane and fuses with it, releasing its contents outside, using energy from ATP. Real-world examples: digestive enzymes released from pancreatic cells (recall 3.1.1's extracellular enzymes by label); 4.1.4's insulin secretion; plant cells releasing pectin for the cell wall (the S21/22 Q5(b)(ii) context).
- *Comparison, facilitated diffusion versus active transport* (the M24/22 Q1(b)(ii) form): similarity: both move specific substances through transport proteins, carrier proteins in both cases for this comparison (facilitated diffusion may also use channels); a conformational-change similarity is credited only in carrier context, and assigning it to a channel is rejected (M24/22 MS p.6); differences: direction relative to the concentration gradient (down vs against) and energy (no ATP vs ATP from respiration).
- *Saturation* (the S23/21 Q3(b)(i) form): on a graph of rate of uptake against external concentration, simple diffusion keeps rising, while carrier-mediated uptake (facilitated or active) levels off because **the number of carrier proteins in the membrane is limited**; when all are occupied, more substrate outside cannot increase the rate; uptake reaches a plateau (constant), it does not stop or peak. The protein is a carrier protein with a **binding site**, not an enzyme with an active site (same vocabulary guard as E44). Saturation alone is not a universal way to distinguish active from facilitated transport (the S23/21 context is grape hexose transporters inserted into mutant yeast, QP pp.8–9).

Handle for 4.2.1b: *a revolving door* for a carrier protein (VIDEO-STRUCTURE's own example), converted at once: *a carrier protein binds a specific molecule or ion and changes shape to move it across the membrane*; for active transport, *energy from ATP drives that change of shape against the gradient*.

### 4.2.6

**Syllabus verbatim — [p22](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=22):**

> explain the movement of water between cells and solutions in terms of water potential and explain the different effects of the movement of water on plant cells and animal cells (knowledge of solute potential and pressure potential is not expected)

**Commands / content type:** explain. **MECHANISM / COMPARISON**. Budget **8:15**, 11 teaching beats, 0 errors.

**What we teach.** Using the `WaterPotentialModel` from 4.2.1a (recalled by label; this lesson stands alone and re-states the definition): Water potential describes water's tendency to move. Pure water at atmospheric pressure is the reference, with water potential 0 kPa. At the same temperature and pressure, adding solute lowers water potential, so the solutions in this comparison have negative values; a less negative value is higher. Net osmosis is from higher to lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane. Water still crosses both ways when the water potentials are equal. Three cases for each cell type, always stated as a comparison of the **solution's** water potential with the **cell's**, labelled as the **initial** condition:

- *Animal cell (red blood cell)*: solution with a higher water potential than the cell → net water entry → the cell swells and may burst (**haemolysis**: the name is specific to red blood cells; other animal cells may lyse without that name), because there is no cell wall to resist expansion; equal water potentials → no net movement: in this particular equal-potential comparison, a normal red blood cell is shown remaining unchanged (equality by itself does not determine a cell's shape); solution with a lower water potential → net water loss → the cell shrinks (**crenated**: named as the appearance).
- *Plant cell*: higher water potential outside (initially) → net water entry → the vacuole and cytoplasm swell and the protoplast pushes against the cell wall; the cell becomes **turgid**; the wall resists further expansion, so the cell does not burst. As a plant cell takes up water, its expanding contents press against the resisting wall; its water potential rises until it equals that of the surrounding solution and there is no net water entry. Show the net arrow fading at equilibrium while individual water molecules continue crossing both ways. A turgid plant cell can therefore have the same water potential as its surroundings. **Equal water potentials** (the `plant-equal` case) → no net movement: equal water potentials mean no net movement, not necessarily a flaccid cell or equal solute concentrations. Lower water potential outside (initially) → net water loss → the protoplast shrinks, the cell becomes **flaccid**, and with further loss the protoplast withdraws from the wall: **plasmolysis**; the wall is freely permeable, so the space between wall and membrane fills with the external solution.
- The difference in outcome between the two cell types comes from the **cell wall** (the SME AL 4.2.12 ceiling's emphasis, and the syllabus's "different effects").

Explained without component potentials: "the wall resists expansion" and "the cell's water potential rises until it equals the solution's" are said; no component-potential names or equations are introduced (the wall mechanism and changing total water potential suffice at syllabus depth; no calculation). Framing: cells must keep their water content in a working range; plants use turgor for support (a wilting lettuce crisping in water: a turgor demonstration, not a water-potential measurement); for animal cells, an isotonic saline example shows why matching the effective osmotic conditions around red blood cells can prevent large net changes in their volume (no prescription, universal IV-fluid claim or clinical digression).

**Real-world samples.** Blood cells are shown as a model, not handled; if a micrograph of red blood cells in solutions is used, it is **sourced**, never generated (asset dependency). Lettuce crisping and wilting: an explain-beat example; what it shows (net water entry restores turgidity) and where it stops (it does not measure water potential).
