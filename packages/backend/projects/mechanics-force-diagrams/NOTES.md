# Force diagrams — five-minute revision

## The particle model

A force diagram shows only the forces acting **on one chosen body**. Model that body as a particle: replace its shape by one point or small box and draw every force arrow from that point. The real places where the forces act are ignored, so turning effects are not considered.

Make a separate force diagram for each body. Do not put a force that acts on a table, wall, string or second particle onto the chosen body’s diagram.

## Force checklist

Ask about these forces every time:

- **Weight:** if the body has mass \(m\), draw \(W=mg\) vertically down. For Cambridge 9709 Mechanics, use \(g=10\text{ m s}^{-2}\) unless the question gives another value.
- **Normal contact force:** if a surface touches the body, draw \(R\) perpendicular to the surface and away from it. A smooth surface may still exert \(R\); “smooth” means there is no friction.
- **Friction:** at a rough contact, draw \(F\) parallel to the surface, opposing relative sliding or the tendency to slide. Decide the likely slipping direction before choosing the arrow direction.
- **Tension:** a taut string pulls. Draw \(T\) along the string, away from the body.
- **Thrust:** a compressed rod or connector pushes. Draw the thrust along the connector, towards the body.
- **Air resistance:** for a body moving through air, draw the resistance opposite its velocity relative to the air. It may be labelled \(D\), \(F\), or with the symbol given in the question.

Also include any stated applied, driving or braking force. Do not invent a force merely because the body is moving: motion does not require a forward resultant.

## Arrow conventions

1. Draw every force arrow **from the particle**.
2. Point it in the direction in which that force acts on the chosen body.
3. Make its length approximately represent the force’s size when magnitudes are known; equal forces should look equal and a larger force should have a longer arrow. The diagram need not be exactly to scale unless told otherwise.
4. Label the arrow with a symbol or known magnitude, such as \(R\), \(T\), \(F\), \(mg\), or \(12\text{ N}\).
5. Keep motion, velocity and acceleration arrows visually separate. They are not forces and do not belong in the force count.

A force arrow shows what acts on the particle, not where the particle is travelling. For example, a car moving right can have a resultant force to the left while braking.

## Common situations

### Box on a horizontal table

For a box at rest on a horizontal table, draw:

- \(mg\) vertically down;
- \(R\) vertically up.

If there is no vertical acceleration, these balance:

\(R=mg\)

Do not add friction unless the contact is rough **and** there is a force or tendency that friction must oppose.

### Hanging mass

For a mass hanging freely from one taut vertical string, draw:

- \(mg\) down;
- \(T\) up, along the string.

There is no normal reaction because there is no contact surface. If the mass is in equilibrium, \(T=mg\).

### Block on a rough slope

For a block on a plane inclined at angle \(\theta\), draw:

- \(mg\) vertically down, not perpendicular to the plane;
- \(R\) perpendicular to the plane;
- \(F\) parallel to the plane and opposite the actual or impending relative motion.

If the block would otherwise slide down the slope, friction acts up the slope. Resolve weight only after completing the force diagram:

\(\text{component down the slope}=mg\sin\theta\)

\(\text{component into the slope}=mg\cos\theta\)

The components are calculation aids; do not draw \(mg\), \(mg\sin\theta\), and \(mg\cos\theta\) as three separate forces.

### Car braking on a level road

For a car moving right and braking, a suitable particle diagram may contain:

- \(mg\) down;
- \(R\) up;
- a braking force to the left;
- air resistance or other specified resistance to the left.

If the engine is no longer driving, do not leave a forward driving force on the diagram. Avoid double-counting tyre-road friction: if the question calls it the braking force, it is not a second independent friction force as well.

## Equilibrium check

A particle is in equilibrium when the resultant force is zero:

\(\sum \mathbf F=\mathbf 0\)

In two dimensions, resolve in two perpendicular directions, usually horizontal/vertical or parallel/perpendicular to a slope:

\(\sum F_x=0,\qquad \sum F_y=0\)

Equilibrium includes rest and constant-velocity motion. A moving particle can therefore have balanced forces.

## Worked example 1: box on a table

A \(5\text{ kg}\) box is at rest on a rough horizontal table. A horizontal pull of \(18\text{ N}\) acts to the right. Draw and find all forces, taking \(g=10\text{ m s}^{-2}\).

The diagram has four arrows from the particle:

- weight \(5g=50\text{ N}\) down;
- normal reaction \(R\) up;
- pull \(18\text{ N}\) right;
- friction \(F\) left, because the pull tends to move the box right.

Vertical equilibrium gives

\(R-50=0\)

so

\(R=50\text{ N}\)

Horizontal equilibrium gives

\(18-F=0\)

so

\(F=18\text{ N}\)

Friction is not automatically \(\mu R\). That equality applies only when friction is limiting; here equilibrium determines the required value.

## Worked example 2: block on a rough slope

A \(4\text{ kg}\) block rests on a rough plane inclined at \(30^\circ\) to the horizontal. It has a tendency to slip down the plane. Find the normal reaction and friction, taking \(g=10\text{ m s}^{-2}\).

Draw exactly three forces: \(40\text{ N}\) vertically down, \(R\) perpendicular to the plane, and \(F\) up the plane.

Perpendicular to the plane,

\(R-40\cos30^\circ=0\)

so

\(R=20\sqrt3\text{ N}\)

Parallel to the plane,

\(F-40\sin30^\circ=0\)

so

\(F=20\text{ N}\)

The words “tendency to slip down” determine that friction points up the plane.

## Self-check

An \(800\text{ kg}\) car travels to the right on a level road and brakes. The engine provides no driving force. Air resistance is \(300\text{ N}\), and the braking force is \(1700\text{ N}\). Taking \(g=10\text{ m s}^{-2}\), list the force arrows and find the resultant force.

### Answer

Draw \(8000\text{ N}\) down for weight, \(R\) up, \(300\text{ N}\) left for air resistance, and \(1700\text{ N}\) left for braking. With no vertical acceleration,

\(R=8000\text{ N}\)

The horizontal resultant is

\(300+1700=2000\text{ N}\)

to the left. Do not add a rightward force merely because the car is still moving right.

## Common exam slips

- Drawing forces exerted **by** the chosen body instead of forces acting **on** it.
- Drawing one combined diagram for several particles instead of isolating each particle.
- Forgetting weight because the question does not mention it explicitly.
- Drawing weight perpendicular to a slope instead of vertically down.
- Drawing the normal reaction vertically instead of perpendicular to the contact surface.
- Adding a normal reaction to a freely hanging body with no surface contact.
- Assuming a smooth surface has no normal reaction; it has no friction.
- Making friction oppose velocity automatically instead of opposing relative slip or its tendency.
- Drawing tension towards a particle or thrust away from it.
- Adding a “force of motion”, a continuing “projection force”, or an acceleration arrow as though it were a force.
- Replacing weight by its components and then also keeping the original \(mg\) arrow.
- Writing \(F=\mu R\) when friction is not stated to be limiting.
- Assuming equal and opposite vertical forces mean full equilibrium while horizontal forces remain unbalanced.
- Using \(g=9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}\) when the Cambridge question expects \(g=10\text{ m s}^{-2}\), or using \(10\) when another value is given.
