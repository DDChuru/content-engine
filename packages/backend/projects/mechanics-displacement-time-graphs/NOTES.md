# Displacement-time graphs - five-minute revision

## What the graph records

A displacement-time graph tracks a particle's signed position relative to a fixed origin. Time is on the horizontal axis and displacement, usually \(s\) or \(x\), is on the vertical axis. A point \((t,s)\) therefore answers: “Where is the particle at this instant?”

Positive and negative do not mean near and far. They identify opposite sides of the origin according to the chosen positive direction. A point below the time axis has negative displacement; the particle itself has not travelled a “negative distance.” Whenever \(s=0\), the particle is at the origin.

## Gradient means velocity

Between two points on a straight section,

\(v=\dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}\)

The sign gives the direction:

- positive gradient: \(v>0\), so displacement is increasing;
- negative gradient: \(v<0\), so displacement is decreasing;
- zero gradient: \(v=0\), so the particle is at rest.

The magnitude of the gradient gives the speed. A steeper line has a larger value of \(|v|\), so the particle is moving faster. A straight line has one unchanging gradient and therefore constant velocity. A horizontal straight line is the special case of constant velocity \(0\).

On a curve, the gradient changes, so velocity changes. To find the instantaneous velocity at one point, draw a tangent there and calculate its gradient using two well-separated points on the tangent:

\(v=\dfrac{\text{vertical change on tangent}}{\text{horizontal change on tangent}}\)

Do not decide “speeding up” from curvature alone. Compare the velocity's sign with how its magnitude changes. For example, a negative gradient becoming more negative means the particle is moving faster in the negative direction.

## Displacement and distance

Displacement over an interval uses only the endpoints:

\(\text{net displacement}=s_{\text{final}}-s_{\text{initial}}\)

Total distance adds every piece of ground covered. Split the graph at each turning point, then add the absolute changes in displacement:

\(\text{total distance}=|\Delta s_1|+|\Delta s_2|+\cdots\)

Distance from the origin at one instant is \(|s|\). It is different from total distance travelled, which never decreases as a journey continues.

Average velocity keeps direction; average speed does not:

\(\text{average velocity}=\dfrac{s_{\text{final}}-s_{\text{initial}}}{\text{total time}}\)

\(\text{average speed}=\dfrac{\text{total distance travelled}}{\text{total time}}\)

Average velocity may be positive, negative or zero. Average speed is non-negative.

## Worked examples

### 1. Constant velocity from a straight line

A straight segment joins \((0\text{ s},-2\text{ m})\) to \((5\text{ s},13\text{ m})\).

\(v=\dfrac{13-(-2)}{5-0}=\dfrac{15}{5}=3\text{ m s}^{-1}\)

The positive result means motion in the chosen positive direction. The particle begins \(2\text{ m}\) on the negative side of the origin; that does not alter the gradient calculation.

### 2. A journey that returns past the origin

At \(t=0\text{ s}\), a particle is at \(s=2\text{ m}\). It moves in a straight graph segment to \(s=10\text{ m}\) at \(t=4\text{ s}\), rests until \(t=6\text{ s}\), then reaches \(s=-6\text{ m}\) at \(t=10\text{ s}\).

First velocity:

\(v_1=\dfrac{10-2}{4-0}=2\text{ m s}^{-1}\)

During the horizontal section:

\(v_2=0\text{ m s}^{-1}\)

Final velocity:

\(v_3=\dfrac{-6-10}{10-6}=-4\text{ m s}^{-1}\)

The last section crosses \(s=0\), so the particle passes the origin and continues into the negative region. For the whole journey,

\(\text{net displacement}=-6-2=-8\text{ m}\)

\(\text{total distance}=|10-2|+|-6-10|=8+16=24\text{ m}\)

\(\text{average velocity}=\dfrac{-8}{10}=-0.8\text{ m s}^{-1}\)

\(\text{average speed}=\dfrac{24}{10}=2.4\text{ m s}^{-1}\)

### 3. Instantaneous velocity from a tangent

At \(t=4\text{ s}\), a curved graph is at \(s=9\text{ m}\). A tangent drawn at that point passes through the convenient grid points \((2\text{ s},3\text{ m})\) and \((6\text{ s},15\text{ m})\).

\(v(4)=\dfrac{15-3}{6-2}=\dfrac{12}{4}=3\text{ m s}^{-1}\)

This is the velocity at \(t=4\text{ s}\), not the average velocity from \(t=2\text{ s}\) to \(t=6\text{ s}\). Those two times are merely points chosen on the tangent line.

### 4. Read total distance, displacement and averages

A graph passes through \((0\text{ s},0\text{ m})\), \((3\text{ s},12\text{ m})\), \((8\text{ s},4\text{ m})\) and \((10\text{ s},4\text{ m})\), with straight segments between the points.

The final position is \(4\text{ m}\), so

\(\text{net displacement}=4-0=4\text{ m}\)

The particle travels \(12\text{ m}\) outward, \(8\text{ m}\) back, then rests:

\(\text{total distance}=12+8+0=20\text{ m}\)

\(\text{average velocity}=\dfrac{4}{10}=0.4\text{ m s}^{-1}\)

\(\text{average speed}=\dfrac{20}{10}=2.0\text{ m s}^{-1}\)

## Quick self-check

1. What does a point below the time axis mean?
2. A straight section falls from \(s=14\text{ m}\) at \(t=2\text{ s}\) to \(s=2\text{ m}\) at \(t=5\text{ s}\). Find the velocity.
3. What does a horizontal section show?
4. A particle moves from \(s=-3\text{ m}\) to \(s=5\text{ m}\), then back to \(s=1\text{ m}\). Find its total distance and net displacement.
5. Why is a tangent needed on a curved displacement-time graph?

### Answers

1. The particle is on the negative side of the chosen origin; its distance from the origin is \(|s|\).
2. \(v=\dfrac{2-14}{5-2}=-4\text{ m s}^{-1}\).
3. The displacement is unchanged, so \(v=0\text{ m s}^{-1}\) and the particle is at rest.
4. \(\text{total distance}=|5-(-3)|+|1-5|=8+4=12\text{ m}\), while \(\text{net displacement}=1-(-3)=4\text{ m}\).
5. The graph has no single gradient over the whole curve. The tangent's gradient gives the instantaneous velocity at the chosen point.

## Common exam slips

- Treating a displacement-time graph like a velocity-time graph. On an \(s\)-\(t\) graph, gradient gives velocity; area under the graph has no standard kinematics meaning.
- Reading the final vertical coordinate as distance travelled. It is displacement from the origin; total distance requires adding absolute changes across every leg.
- Thinking a negative displacement means a negative distance, or that it automatically means negative velocity.
- Saying every upward curve means “accelerating.” Speed change depends on the signs and magnitudes of the gradients.
- Using two points on the curve rather than two points on the tangent when finding instantaneous velocity.
- Forgetting units on a gradient. Displacement divided by time gives units such as \(\text{m s}^{-1}\), not metres or \(\text{m s}^{-2}\).
- Assuming the graph starts at the origin. Always read the initial displacement from the vertical axis.
