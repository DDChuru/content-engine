**Counting convention, held constant across the topic (the chromosome/chromatid trap).** *Count chromosomes by counting centromeres.* Before DNA replication, a chromosome contains one long DNA molecule associated with histone proteins. After replication, it contains two sister chromatids, each with its own DNA molecule associated with histones — still **one** chromosome. When the centromere divides at anaphase, each separated chromatid has its own centromere and is from then on a **daughter chromosome**. Counts must state their compartment: whole cell, one pole, one nucleus or one daughter cell. In telophase before cytokinesis is complete, the model has eight chromosomes in the whole cell but four in each new nucleus. Use the DNA-molecule counter throughout; after sister separation, relabel the separated units as daughter chromosomes rather than implying their DNA has disappeared. A typical diploid human somatic cell is the human reference, not every human cell. In the topic's model cell (2n = 4): G1 = 4 chromosomes / 4 DNA molecules (whole cell); after S through metaphase = 4 chromosomes / 8 DNA molecules (8 chromatids); after sister separation (anaphase, and telophase before cytokinesis) = 8 chromosomes / 8 DNA molecules in the whole cell, 4 at each pole; each daughter nucleus = 4 chromosomes / 4 DNA molecules. Typical diploid human somatic cell: 46/46, 46/92, 92/92 (whole cell after separation), 46/46 per daughter nucleus. Every storyboard that shows a count uses this rule, these numbers and a named compartment.

**Wording held constant (the "splitting" trap).** At anaphase, the centromeres divide and sister chromatids separate to opposite poles. Once separated, each is a daughter chromosome. Use “sister chromatids separate” when explaining the transition; “daughter chromosomes move to opposite poles” is also correct. The error is sending an intact two-chromatid chromosome to a pole, not using the word chromosome after separation. Do not introduce a meiosis comparison here. "Splits" may be used as the everyday verb of the **centromere**; never "the chromosome splits in half"; never "chromosomes form/replicate in prophase" (they **condense** and become visible; they were replicated in S phase). "Nuclear envelope" is the taught term (syllabus wording); the w22_23 reject of "nuclear membrane" is local (E5-03). Mitosis is **nuclear division**; cytokinesis is **division of the cytoplasm**; the mitotic cell cycle is interphase + mitosis + cytokinesis. The two are defined as distinct processes; cytokinesis commonly begins during late mitosis, so "cytokinesis follows mitosis" is said as the usual order, not as a rule that they never overlap.

### 5.1.1

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](/home/dachu/sme-9700-archive/syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> describe the structure of a chromosome, limited to:
>
> - DNA
> - histone proteins
> - sister chromatids
> - centromere
> - telomeres

**Commands / content type:** describe. **STRUCTURE**. Budget **6:00**, 11 beats, **0 errors**. Build position 1; publishes `ChromosomeModel`.

**What we teach:** Before DNA replication, a chromosome contains one long DNA molecule associated with histone proteins. After replication, it contains two sister chromatids, each with its own DNA molecule associated with histones. The DNA–histone packing is what lets the DNA of one cell fit inside the nucleus (real-world scale: the DNA in one typical human cell, laid end to end, is about two metres long — stated as typical, and shown as a scale bar against a nucleus a few micrometres across). In a non-dividing cell the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out; before division they condense (coil more tightly), becoming short and thick enough to see with a light microscope. After the DNA has been replicated (5.1.3 owns *when*), a chromosome consists of **two sister chromatids**, each one DNA molecule, genetically identical because one is a copy of the other, joined at the **centromere**. **Telomeres** are the ends of each chromatid: repeated DNA sequences that do not code for a protein (their *role* is 5.1.4's; here they are located and named). The counting convention above is taught here, on two states of the same model: unreplicated (one chromatid) and replicated (two sister chromatids). The syllabus list is a ceiling: no nucleosome nomenclature, no chromatin fibre levels, no banding, no karyotyping, no homologous pairs (16.1.2).

### 5.1.2

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](/home/dachu/sme-9700-archive/syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> explain the importance of mitosis in the production of genetically identical daughter cells during:
>
> - growth of multicellular organisms
> - replacement of damaged or dead cells
> - repair of tissues by cell replacement
> - asexual reproduction

**Commands / content type:** explain. **CAUSAL / PURPOSE**. Budget **6:30**, 10 beats, **1 error (E5-06)**. Build position 6 (it explains *why identical*, which needs replication (5.1.3) and separation of sister chromatids (5.2.1); it opens on its own labelled recall of both and stands alone).

**What we teach:** Why *identical* matters: Mitosis supplies newly formed daughter nuclei with the same genetic information, so new cells can support growth, replacement and repair. Some cells later specialise and stop dividing; mature human red blood cells even lose their nuclei. Genetic identity does not itself specify a cell's specialised function. Mitosis delivers to each daughter nucleus the same number of chromosomes as the parent, carrying the same genetic information, **because** each chromosome was replicated into two identical sister chromatids in S phase **and** one chromatid of every chromosome goes to each pole in anaphase (G05's check: "Link replication to two copies and segregation to one equivalent set per nucleus", an authored inference; the plan check found identical sisters, alignment and equal distribution supported in the MS). Then the four contexts, each with one real-world example and the reason identical cells serve it: **growth** (a root tip — mitosis supplies new cells for growth; root lengthening also depends on those cells elongating, so mitosis is not said to produce all the length; a child's growing tissues — new cells carry the same genes as the tissue they join); **replacement of damaged or dead cells** (the lining of the small intestine and the outer skin are continually worn away and replaced by cells produced by mitosis — "typical"); **repair of tissues by cell replacement** (Mitosis supplies replacement cells during tissue repair; where needed, daughter cells differentiate into the appropriate specialised types — a skin cut is the example, without claiming every tissue is restored perfectly); **asexual reproduction** (a strawberry plant's runners grow new plantlets whose cells are all produced by mitosis, so the offspring are genetically identical to the parent — a clone). The four contexts are woven, not listed (VIDEO-STRUCTURE "do not let the list become bullets"). Which cells do the dividing in replacement and repair is handed to 5.1.5 by label ("stem cells — next").

**Exact mark-scheme keys:** [w22_23 Q4(b), QP PDF p11 / MS PDF p16](/home/dachu/sme-9700-archive/pastpapers/2022/November/9700_w22_ms_23.pdf#page=16) — asks for **two events**; marked out of **3, any three listed points**; identical sisters, alignment and equal distribution are supported (plan check A04: VERIFIED SUMMARY, not a verbatim MS or candidate quote). ### 5.1.3

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](/home/dachu/sme-9700-archive/syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> outline the mitotic cell cycle, including:
>
> - interphase (growth in G1 and G2 phases and DNA replication in S phase)
> - mitosis
> - cytokinesis

**Commands / content type:** outline. **PROCESS / DATA (graph)**. Budget **8:30**, 14 beats, **2 errors (E5-01, E5-02)**. Build position 2; publishes `CellCycleWheel` and `DNAContentGraph`.

**What we teach:** The cycle as a loop a dividing cell goes round: **interphase** — G1 (the cell grows: makes proteins and organelles), S (DNA replication: every chromosome becomes two sister chromatids), G2 (further growth and preparation for division); then **mitosis** (nuclear division: one nucleus → two genetically identical nuclei) and **cytokinesis** (division of the cytoplasm → two cells). Interphase is typically the longest part of the cycle (said as typical; the wheel's arc lengths are labelled schematic). Handle: *the cycle is a copy-then-share routine* — immediately converted: "Written properly: DNA is replicated during the S phase of interphase; mitosis then separates the copies into two nuclei; cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm." The per-cell graph is the teaching trace. Other axes exist (the lesson teaches the student to read the axis label first): a graph of DNA per nucleus / per chromosome set halves when the two new nuclei form — during open mitosis there is temporarily no intact nucleus, so that interval is marked schematically. Further 5.1.3 evidence (plan check): s23_12 Q21 (key B) contrasts one copy in G1 with two at the start of cytokinesis — nuclear division is complete before the cytoplasm divides. The chromosomes are present throughout interphase, long and thin and not individually visible; replication does not happen when they become visible.

### 5.1.4

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](/home/dachu/sme-9700-archive/syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> outline the role of telomeres in preventing the loss of genes from the ends of chromosomes during DNA replication

**Commands / content type:** outline. **MECHANISM (bounded)**. Budget **4:00**, 8 beats, **0 errors**. Build position 3; publishes `TelomereEndModel`.

**What we teach:** In typical dividing somatic cells, chromosome ends can shorten over successive rounds of DNA replication. Telomeres contain repeated non-coding DNA, so shortening initially removes telomeric sequence rather than genes near the ends. This protects genes during repeated replication; it does not mean telomeres prevent all DNA loss or all DNA damage. Some cells maintain their telomeres. No telomerase mechanism is required here. The general replication process is taught in 6.1.4; the detailed problem of copying chromosome ends is outside this lesson's required mechanism. **Telomeres** are long stretches of a short, repeated, non-coding DNA sequence at the ends of chromosomes (in humans the repeat is TTAGGG — a real-world detail, stated once, identifying the repeat without quantifying the amount lost). The handle is a *buffer at the end of the line*, immediately converted: 'Written properly: telomeres are repeated non-coding DNA at the ends of chromosomes, so the shortening that happens over repeated replication removes telomere, not genes.' A labelled **thought experiment** ('what if there were no telomere?') shows the shortening endpoint reaching a gene band over successive rounds; it is captioned *thought experiment — not a real chromosome* throughout, and returns to the real state.

### 5.1.6

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](/home/dachu/sme-9700-archive/syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> explain how uncontrolled cell division can result in the formation of a tumour

**Commands / content type:** explain. **CAUSAL**. Budget **6:00**, 9 beats, **1 error (E5-07)**. Build position 8; publishes `TissueGrowthModel`.

**What we teach:** In a healthy adult tissue that is maintaining its size, the rate of mitosis is controlled so that new cells replace lost ones (developing tissues grow, so they need net growth). Genes control when a cell divides. A **mutation** in a gene that controls cell division can make a cell divide repeatedly when it should not; its daughter cells inherit the mutation and also divide without control; the result is a mass of cells, a **tumour**. Said as typical: usually more than one such mutation has accumulated in the same cell line before a tumour forms. A **benign** tumour typically stays in one place and does not invade surrounding tissue; a **malignant** tumour (a cancer) invades surrounding tissue, and cells can break away, travel in the blood or lymph and form secondary tumours elsewhere. Real-world: ultraviolet radiation in sunlight can cause mutations in skin cells — one typical mutagen, stated once (no oncology catalogue). The chain is causal, clause by clause: mutation → loss of control of cell division → repeated/uncontrolled mitosis → mass of cells → tumour. Mitosis itself is not the fault; **uncontrolled** mitosis is.

**Exact mark-scheme keys:** [w20_21 Q6(a)(i), QP p15 / MS PDF p12](/home/dachu/sme-9700-archive/pastpapers/2020/November/9700_w20_ms_21.pdf#page=12) — mutation context supplied; **2 marks, any two credited consequences** (A10, VERIFIED SUMMARY; optional oncogene/checkpoint alternatives in the MS do not become required teaching). **Scope ceiling and boundaries:** no oncogene/tumour-suppressor terms, no checkpoint proteins, no treatments, no named cancers beyond the one typical mutagen; "a tumour is not always a cancer" is taught (benign vs malignant), and "cancer is caused by one mutation" is never said.

### 5.2.1

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](/home/dachu/sme-9700-archive/syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> describe the behaviour of chromosomes in plant and animal cells during the mitotic cell cycle and the associated behaviour of the nuclear envelope, the cell surface membrane and the spindle (names of the main stages of mitosis are expected: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase)

**Commands / content type:** describe. **PROCESS / MECHANISM (motion-heavy)**. Budget **12:00**, 19 beats, **2 errors (E5-03, E5-04)**. Build position 4; publishes `MitosisCellModel` (animal and plant) and the spindle.

**What we teach:** One model cell (2n = 4) run through the cycle twice — animal, then plant — with the chromosome counts on screen.
- **Interphase (recalled from 5.1.3 by label):** nuclear envelope intact; chromosomes long and thin, already replicated by G2.
- **Prophase:** chromosomes condense (shorten and thicken) and become visible, each as two sister chromatids joined at the centromere; in animal cells the centrosomes (each with a pair of centrioles) move towards opposite poles; the spindle of microtubules forms; the nucleolus disappears; by the end of prophase the **nuclear envelope breaks down** into fragments.
- **Metaphase:** spindle fibres attach to each chromosome at its centromere; the chromosomes are moved to line up across the equator of the spindle, one chromosome's centromere at a time, each chromatid facing a different pole.
- **Anaphase:** the **centromeres divide**; the sister chromatids separate and are pulled to opposite poles, centromere first (in this schematic, the arms trail behind the leading centromere; real chromosomes may look U- or V-shaped), as the spindle fibres attached to them shorten; each pole receives one chromatid of every chromosome. Once separated, each is a **daughter chromosome** (whole cell: 8 in the model, 92 in a typical human somatic cell; 4 / 46 at each pole).
- **Telophase:** the daughter chromosomes arrive at the poles and uncoil (become long and thin again); a **nuclear envelope re-forms** around each group; the nucleolus reappears in each new nucleus; the spindle breaks down. Two genetically identical nuclei — the whole undivided cell still holds 8 chromosomes, 4 in each new nucleus.
- **Cytokinesis (plant and animal differ):** animal — the **cell surface membrane** is drawn inwards around the middle (a cleavage furrow) until the cell is pinched into two; plant — the cell wall prevents pinching, so vesicles gather at the equator and fuse to form a **cell plate**, which becomes the new cell walls (with cell surface membranes on each side) between the two cells. Plant cells have no centrioles, yet still form a spindle — said as the typical difference.
Handle: *line up, split the pair, walk apart* — converted at once into the credited sentence for each stage. Spindle-fibre attachment point: "centromere" is the taught term; "kinetochore" may appear once as an on-screen alternative label for the attachment region (accepted in s21_22 Q1(a)(ii), ignored in s23_21 Q4(c)(i) — never required).

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](/home/dachu/sme-9700-archive/syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> interpret photomicrographs, diagrams and microscope slides of cells in different stages of the mitotic cell cycle and identify the main stages of mitosis

**Commands / content type:** interpret; identify. **PRACTICAL / IMAGE INTERPRETATION**. Budget **11:00**, 17 beats, **1 error (E5-05)**. Build position 5; publishes `RootTipSquashRig` and `FieldOfViewSchematic`; reuses the Topic 1 light-microscope model and handling standard.

**What we teach — three parts.**

*(a) Identification from chromosome evidence (the examined skill), on diagrams **and real photomicrographs**.* Teach with diagrams and at least one sourced, licensed real photomicrograph set showing the relevant stage features, plus the slide-preparation/viewing demonstration. A schematic supports interpretation but cannot replace photomicrograph coverage. Record source, licence, organism and stain before the storyboard is cleared for production. The decision sequence the student runs on any image: Examine cell boundaries and the number and positions of nuclei/chromosome groups first. A clearly resolved single nucleus with diffuse chromatin supports interphase; two reforming nuclei within one dividing cell support telophase/cytokinesis. Use chromosome arrangement to distinguish prophase, metaphase and anaphase, allowing for viewing angle. If resolution or overlap prevents a justified identification, say so; lack of visible detail alone does not prove interphase. (Condensed chromosomes spread through the nuclear region, not aligned → prophase; chromosomes arranged across the equator → metaphase — a plate seen from another orientation may not look like the model's straight line; two groups of daughter chromosomes moving to opposite poles → anaphase.) Do not demand a spindle or a resolved nuclear-envelope double line in every light micrograph: s21_22's question explicitly says its microtubules are not visible. The student commits to **one** stage per cell and names the visible feature that justifies it (G05 checkpoint 1: "identify a stage from a new diagram, then identify the visible evidence supporting it" — G05's words; the papers ask identification only, so the justification is our diagnostic teaching addition). The same `MitosisCellModel` plant states are the diagram reference.

*(b) The root-tip squash (microscope slides).* **Real-world sample, the REAL-WORLD SAMPLES rule applied:** garlic (*Allium sativum*) cloves stood in water until roots grow. **What in it the method responds to:** toluidine blue stains chromatin deep blue; condensed mitotic chromosomes show as distinct dark shapes. Toluidine blue reveals chromatin in interphase nuclei as well as condensed mitotic chromosomes; staining alone does not identify a dividing cell. We identify stages from the arrangement of visible chromosomes and nuclei. **Does it fit the method:** yes, typically — the root tip's **meristem**, just behind the root cap, is a region of active mitosis; garlic has relatively few, large chromosomes (the 2n = 4 teaching cell is a simplified model, not the garlic karyotype); root tips contain no chlorophyll, so no green pigment competes with the stain. **Limits / interferences:** The small sampled tip includes a root cap: select the meristem behind it, rather than assuming every cell in the terminal piece is dividing. Cells further back divide less often; a squash that is not spread thin hides chromosomes behind other cells. These are prepared specimens: no living mitosis continues under the coverslip.

**Method (the one complete recipe used, from SAPS; no splicing with other protocols):** Use the SAPS fresh-garlic method: pre-equilibrate 1 mol dm⁻³ HCl at 40 °C for 15 minutes. Support the clove so its roots enter the acid; start the five-minute treatment timer at contact. Remove and rinse the roots. Cut the terminal 3 mm onto a watch glass; stain for two minutes with one drop of 1% aqueous toluidine blue. Remove excess stain and rinse. Transfer gently with a paintbrush to a clean slide, add water and tease apart with a mounted needle. Lower the coverslip; wrap the slide in paper towel and press gently vertically on a flat supported surface, without sliding or twisting. Locate the meristem at low power, then inspect chromosomes at high power. Eye protection, careful handling of acid and sharp tools, and gentle pressure to avoid broken glass remain visible precautions. (Source: SAPS, *A level set practicals: microscopy of root tip mitosis*, resource 1358, student and technical sheets, read by the plan check.) **Why the acid:** Acid treatment softens the material between neighbouring cells, helping stain penetration and allowing the cells to spread into a thin layer. These are prepared specimens; do not animate living mitosis continuing under the coverslip or describe acid contact as instantaneous fixation. **Real colours:** Show dark-blue chromatin with a paler background, checked against the selected toluidine-blue preparation. Colours and contrast vary with preparation; identify stages by structure, not hue. Do not use a differently stained reference as the colour standard for this preparation. Root tips start creamy white; the stain darkens in place (intensity only) and never passes through an unrelated colour. **Time compression:** the 15-minute acid pre-warm, the five-minute acid treatment and the two-minute stain are shown with a labelled time-compression device (timer running visibly from first contact, then a *time compressed* tag), never in real time. **Hazards:** 1 mol dm⁻³ HCl at 40 °C (eye protection, no skin contact, the acid container stays supported in the bath, never held over the face); toluidine blue stains skin and clothing (gloves); scalpel/blade and mounted needle handled away from fingers; gentle vertical pressure through the towel to avoid breaking the slide or coverslip. This is **1.1.1's temporary-preparation outcome applied** ("make temporary preparations of cellular material suitable for viewing with a light microscope", [p15](/home/dachu/sme-9700-archive/syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=15)) — the syllabus supports the microscopy skill but does not prescribe garlic, stain, acid concentration or timing — and is shown as a demonstration; hands-on time remains the learner's own ([p51](/home/dachu/sme-9700-archive/syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=51); demonstrations are excluded from the suggested hands-on allocation).
