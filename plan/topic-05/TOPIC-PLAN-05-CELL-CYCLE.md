Counts in the 5.2.2 counting segment are counts **of our drawn schematic field**, drawn as tallies in a results table, never as instrument readings. Cambridge artwork is never reproduced; question text is quoted only where the evidence files hold it.

**Counting convention, held constant across the topic (the chromosome/chromatid trap).** *Count chromosomes by counting centromeres.* A chromosome before S phase is one chromatid (one DNA molecule); after S phase it is two **sister chromatids** (two identical DNA molecules) joined at one centromere — still **one** chromosome. When the centromere divides at anaphase, each chromatid has its own centromere and is from then on counted as a chromosome. In the topic's model cell (2n = 4): G1 = 4 chromosomes, 4 DNA molecules; G2 to metaphase = 4 chromosomes, 8 chromatids; anaphase = 8 chromosomes (4 moving to each pole); each daughter nucleus = 4 chromosomes. The human figures (46; 92 chromatids; 92 separating at anaphase; 46 per daughter nucleus) are used as the real-world anchor. Every storyboard that shows a count uses this rule and these numbers.

**Wording held constant (the "splitting" trap).** In anaphase *the centromere divides* ("splits" may be used as the everyday verb, but only of the **centromere**) and *the sister chromatids separate and are pulled to opposite poles*. Never "the chromosome splits in half", never "the chromosomes separate" for anaphase (that phrase describes homologous chromosomes in meiosis, Topic 16), never "chromosomes form/replicate in prophase" (they **condense** and become visible; they were replicated in S phase). "Nuclear envelope", not "nuclear membrane" (E5-03). Mitosis is **nuclear division**; cytokinesis is **division of the cytoplasm**; the mitotic cell cycle is interphase + mitosis + cytokinesis.

## Outcomes in syllabus order

### 5.1.1

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> describe the structure of a chromosome, limited to:
>
> - DNA
> - histone proteins
> - sister chromatids
> - centromere
> - telomeres

**Commands / content type:** describe. **STRUCTURE**. Budget **6:00**, 11 beats, **0 errors**. Build position 1; publishes `ChromosomeModel`.

**What we teach:** A chromosome is one very long DNA molecule wound around **histone proteins**; the DNA–histone packing is what lets the DNA of one cell fit inside the nucleus (real-world scale: the DNA in one typical human cell, laid end to end, is about two metres long — stated as typical, and shown as a scale bar against a nucleus a few micrometres across). In a non-dividing cell the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out; before division they condense (coil more tightly), becoming short and thick enough to see with a light microscope. After the DNA has been replicated (5.1.3 owns *when*), a chromosome consists of **two sister chromatids**, each one DNA molecule, genetically identical because one is a copy of the other, joined at the **centromere**. **Telomeres** are the ends of each chromatid: repeated DNA sequences that do not code for a protein (their *role* is 5.1.4's; here they are located and named). The counting convention above is taught here, on two states of the same model: unreplicated (one chromatid) and replicated (two sister chromatids). The syllabus list is a ceiling: no nucleosome nomenclature, no chromatin fibre levels, no banding, no karyotyping, no homologous pairs (16.1.2).

### 5.1.2

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> explain the importance of mitosis in the production of genetically identical daughter cells during:
>
> - growth of multicellular organisms
> - replacement of damaged or dead cells
> - repair of tissues by cell replacement
> - asexual reproduction

**Commands / content type:** explain. **CAUSAL / PURPOSE**. Budget **6:30**, 10 beats, **1 error (E5-06)**. Build position 6 (it explains *why identical*, which needs replication (5.1.3) and separation of sister chromatids (5.2.1); it opens on its own labelled recall of both and stands alone).

**What we teach:** Why *identical* matters: every cell of a multicellular organism needs the complete set of genetic information to function and to keep dividing; mitosis delivers to each daughter nucleus the same number of chromosomes as the parent, carrying the same genetic information, **because** each chromosome was replicated into two identical sister chromatids in S phase **and** one chromatid of every chromosome goes to each pole in anaphase (the G05 credit line: "Link replication to two copies and segregation to one equivalent set per nucleus"). Then the four contexts, each with one real-world example and the reason identical cells serve it: **growth** (a root tip lengthening; a child's bones lengthening — new cells must carry the same genes as the tissue they join); **replacement of damaged or dead cells** (the lining of the small intestine and the outer skin are continually worn away and replaced by cells produced by mitosis — "typical"); **repair of tissues by cell replacement** (a cut in the skin closes as cells produced by mitosis replace the lost cells; the replacements must be the same cell type with the same genes); **asexual reproduction** (a strawberry plant's runners grow new plantlets whose cells are all produced by mitosis, so the offspring are genetically identical to the parent — a clone). The four contexts are woven, not listed (VIDEO-STRUCTURE "do not let the list become bullets"). Which cells do the dividing in replacement and repair is handed to 5.1.5 by label ("stem cells — next").

PDF-UNCHECKED. `UNVERIFIED — the w22_23 Q4(b) marking points verbatim`.

### 5.1.3

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> outline the mitotic cell cycle, including:
>
> - interphase (growth in G1 and G2 phases and DNA replication in S phase)
> - mitosis

**Commands / content type:** outline. **PROCESS / DATA (graph)**. Budget **8:30**, 14 beats, **2 errors (E5-01, E5-02)**. Build position 2; publishes `CellCycleWheel` and `DNAContentGraph`.

**What we teach:** The cycle as a loop a dividing cell goes round: **interphase** — G1 (the cell grows: makes proteins and organelles), S (DNA replication: every chromosome becomes two sister chromatids), G2 (further growth and preparation for division); then **mitosis** (nuclear division: one nucleus → two genetically identical nuclei) and **cytokinesis** (division of the cytoplasm → two cells). Interphase is typically the longest part of the cycle (said as typical; the wheel's arc lengths are labelled schematic). A per-nucleus graph halves at the end of nuclear division instead; the lesson shows which the axis says and teaches the student to read the axis label first. The chromosomes are present throughout interphase, long and thin and not individually visible; replication does not happen when they become visible.

### 5.1.4

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> outline the role of telomeres in preventing the loss of genes from the ends of chromosomes during DNA replication

**Commands / content type:** outline. **MECHANISM (bounded)**. Budget **4:00**, 8 beats, **0 errors**. Build position 3; publishes `TelomereEndModel`.

**What we teach:** Each time DNA is replicated, the very end of each DNA molecule is not completely copied, so the new DNA molecule is slightly shorter at that end (why the end cannot be copied is 6.1.4's leading/lagging-strand content; here it is stated as a fact of the copying enzyme and handed off by code). **Telomeres** are long stretches of a short, repeated, non-coding DNA sequence at the ends of chromosomes (in humans the repeat is TTAGGG — a real-world detail, stated once). Because the lost length comes from the telomere, **genes near the ends are not lost** during replication; telomeres get shorter with successive divisions instead. The handle is a *buffer at the end of the line*, immediately converted: "Written properly: telomeres are repeated non-coding sequences at the ends of chromosomes, so the DNA lost at each replication is telomere and not genes." A labelled **thought experiment** ("what if there were no telomere?") shows a gene band being trimmed; it is captioned "thought experiment — not a real chromosome" throughout, and returns to the real state.

### 5.1.6

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> explain how uncontrolled cell division can result in the formation of a tumour

**Commands / content type:** explain. **CAUSAL**. Budget **6:00**, 9 beats, **1 error (E5-07)**. Build position 8; publishes `TissueGrowthModel`.

**What we teach:** In a healthy tissue, the rate of mitosis is controlled so that new cells replace lost ones and the tissue keeps its size. Genes control when a cell divides. A **mutation** in a gene that controls cell division can make a cell divide repeatedly when it should not; its daughter cells inherit the mutation and also divide without control; the result is a mass of cells, a **tumour**. Said as typical: usually more than one such mutation has accumulated in the same cell line before a tumour forms. A **benign** tumour typically stays in one place and does not invade surrounding tissue; a **malignant** tumour (a cancer) invades surrounding tissue, and cells can break away, travel in the blood or lymph and form secondary tumours elsewhere. Real-world: ultraviolet radiation in sunlight can cause mutations in skin cells — one typical mutagen, stated once (no oncology catalogue). The chain is causal, clause by clause: mutation → loss of control of cell division → repeated/uncontrolled mitosis → mass of cells → tumour. Mitosis itself is not the fault; **uncontrolled** mitosis is.

**Exact mark-scheme keys:** [w20_21 Q6(a)(i), 2 marks, MS PDF p12](pastpapers/2020/November/9700_w20_ms_21.pdf#page=12) — G05: "Relate loss of division control to tumour formation" and check "Connect mutation/altered control to inappropriate division rather than treating any mitosis as a tumour." PDF-UNCHECKED. `UNVERIFIED — w20_21 Q6(a)(i) question context and marking points verbatim`.

**Scope ceiling and boundaries:** no oncogene/tumour-suppressor terms, no checkpoint proteins, no treatments, no named cancers beyond the one typical mutagen; "a tumour is not always a cancer" is taught (benign vs malignant), and "cancer is caused by one mutation" is never said.

### 5.2.1

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> describe the behaviour of chromosomes in plant and animal cells during the mitotic cell cycle and the associated behaviour of the nuclear envelope, the cell surface membrane and the spindle (names of the main stages of mitosis are expected: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase)

**Commands / content type:** describe. **PROCESS / MECHANISM (motion-heavy)**. Budget **12:00**, 19 beats, **2 errors (E5-03, E5-04)**. Build position 4; publishes `MitosisCellModel` (animal and plant) and the spindle.

**What we teach:** One model cell (2n = 4) run through the cycle twice — animal, then plant — with the chromosome counts on screen.
- **Interphase (recalled from 5.1.3 by label):** nuclear envelope intact; chromosomes long and thin, already replicated by G2.
- **Prophase:** chromosomes condense (shorten and thicken) and become visible, each as two sister chromatids joined at the centromere; in animal cells the centrosomes (each with a pair of centrioles) move towards opposite poles; the spindle of microtubules forms; by the end of prophase the **nuclear envelope breaks down** into fragments.
- **Metaphase:** spindle fibres attach to each chromosome at its centromere; the chromosomes are moved to line up across the equator of the spindle, one chromosome's centromere at a time, each chromatid facing a different pole.
- **Anaphase:** the **centromere divides**; the sister chromatids are pulled apart to opposite poles, centromere first (the arms trail, giving a V shape), as the spindle fibres attached to them shorten; each pole receives one chromatid of every chromosome. From here each separated chromatid is counted as a chromosome (8 in the model cell, 92 in a human cell, for this brief stage).
- **Telophase:** the chromosomes arrive at the poles and uncoil (become long and thin again); a **nuclear envelope re-forms** around each group; the spindle breaks down. Two genetically identical nuclei.
- **Cytokinesis (plant and animal differ):** animal — the **cell surface membrane** is drawn inwards around the middle (a cleavage furrow) until the cell is pinched into two; plant — the cell wall prevents pinching, so vesicles gather at the equator and fuse to form a **cell plate**, which becomes the new cell walls (with cell surface membranes on each side) between the two cells. Plant cells have no centrioles, yet still form a spindle — said as the typical difference.
Handle: *line up, split the pair, walk apart* — converted at once into the credited sentence for each stage. Spindle-fibre attachment point: "centromere" is the taught term; "kinetochore" may appear once as an on-screen alternative label for the attachment region (accepted in s21_22 Q1(a)(ii), ignored in s23_21 Q4(c)(i) — never required).

### 5.2.2

**Syllabus verbatim — [p23](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=23):**

> interpret photomicrographs, diagrams and microscope slides of cells in different stages of the mitotic cell cycle and identify the main stages of mitosis

**Commands / content type:** interpret; identify. **PRACTICAL / IMAGE INTERPRETATION**. Budget **11:00**, 17 beats, **1 error (E5-05)**. Build position 5; publishes `RootTipSquashRig` and `FieldOfViewSchematic`; reuses the Topic 1 light-microscope model and handling standard.

**What we teach — three parts.**

*(a) Identification from chromosome evidence (the examined skill).* A decision sequence the student runs on any image, each step tied to a visible feature: Are individual chromosomes visible? No → interphase (a nucleus with an intact envelope, chromatin not resolved into chromosomes). Yes → are they in a line across the middle? → metaphase. Are two groups moving apart, arms trailing towards the equator? → anaphase. Two groups at the poles, nuclear envelopes re-forming, perhaps a cell plate between? → telophase. Condensed chromosomes throughout the nucleus, not aligned? → prophase. The student commits to **one** stage per cell and names the feature that justifies it (G05 checkpoint 1: "identify a stage from a new diagram, then identify the visible evidence supporting it"). Diagrams, a drawn schematic field and (production dependency) a licensed photomicrograph are all used; the same `MitosisCellModel` plant states are the reference.

*(b) The root-tip squash (microscope slides).* **Real-world sample, the REAL-WORLD SAMPLES rule applied:** garlic (*Allium sativum*) root tips grown by standing a clove's base in water for a few days. **What in it the method responds to:** the stain binds to the DNA-rich chromosomes, which take it up far more densely when condensed in mitosis than the spread-out chromatin of an interphase nucleus, so dividing cells stand out. **Does it fit the method:** yes, typically — the root tip's **meristem** (just behind the root cap, within about the last 1–2 mm) is a region of active mitosis; garlic and onion have few (2n = 16), large chromosomes; root tips contain no chlorophyll, so no green pigment competes with the stain. **Limits / interferences:** the root cap and the region further back from the tip have few dividing cells, so only the meristem is counted; a squash that is not flattened to one cell layer hides chromosomes behind other cells. Chosen method (one design, stated as the plan's choice): cut about 5 mm from the tips on a white tile with a scalpel; place in 1 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid pre-warmed to about 60 °C in a water bath for about 5 minutes (**timer starts when the tips enter the acid**) — the acid breaks down the material holding neighbouring cell walls together (the middle lamella) so the cells separate when squashed, and it kills the cells, fixing each at the stage it had reached; rinse in cold water; on a slide, keep only the terminal 1–2 mm; add a drop of **toluidine blue** stain for about 2 minutes; lower a coverslip at an angle (the Topic 1 temporary-preparation handling); fold filter paper over it and press **straight down** with the thumb, never sliding sideways; view at low power to find the region of small, closely packed, square cells, then high power (×400) for chromosomes. **Real colours:** the tips start creamy white; after toluidine blue, chromosomes and nuclei stain dark blue to purple and the cytoplasm pale blue (typical) — the stain darkens in place (same hue, rising intensity) and never passes through another colour. **Hazards, specific to these reagents:** warm hydrochloric acid (eye protection, no skin contact, the tube in a rack in the bath, never held over the face); toluidine blue stains skin and clothing (gloves); scalpel cuts away from the fingers on a tile. This is **1.1.1's temporary-preparation outcome applied** ("make temporary preparations of cellular material suitable for viewing with a light microscope", [p15](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=15)) and is shown as a demonstration; hands-on time remains the learner's own ([p51](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=51)).

*(c) Counting what you see — a bounded plan interpretation.* The syllabus's practical skills include "make accurate observations from specimens including counting numbers of cells or cell organelles" ([p54](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=54)) and "carry out appropriate calculations to simplify or explain data, including means, percentages and rates of change" ([p61](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=61)). So, on the **drawn schematic field** only: count every cell once within a marked boundary, classify each by stage (interphase or one of the four), tally in a results table with headings and no units in the body, and calculate the **mitotic index** = number of cells in mitosis ÷ total number of cells counted (× 100 for a percentage). Interpretation at syllabus depth: most cells are in interphase **because** interphase is typically the longest part of the cycle — for a tissue whose cells are dividing independently, not in step, the proportion of cells in a stage reflects the proportion of the cycle spent in it (said with that condition, never as a law). The counts are ours and labelled "count from the schematic field; teaching data". **No error beat, no gate, no numerical mitotic-index lesson** (calibration DO-NOT-ADD); G05 records that "The selected C3 papers do not establish a credited root-tip preparation/mitotic-index procedure." PDF-UNCHECKED. If the conductor rules the counting segment out, it is cut (about 1:15) without touching the error beat.

PDF-UNCHECKED. Supplementary recognition: w22_13 Q18–21 (key MS p2) — `UNVERIFIED — which of these items are stage recognition, with stems and keys`.

## Known traps to hand the checker

Perform the sentence-level sweep for **all, every, always, only, never, cannot, no, because** on every storyboard. These are author checks, not additional error beats.

| Tempting over-claim or slip | Required treatment |
|---|---|
| Chromosomes and chromatids miscounted; a replicated chromosome called "two chromosomes"; a separated chromatid still called a chromatid. | Count centromeres. 2n = 4 model and human figures fixed in the counting convention above; relabel chromatid → chromosome in the same frame the centromere divides (E5-04 in 5.2.1). |
| DNA "replicated in prophase" or "when the chromosomes appear". | Replicated in S phase of interphase; chromosomes **condense** in prophase (E5-01). |
| "Mitosis" used for the whole cycle, or for cytokinesis. | Mitosis = nuclear division; cytokinesis = division of the cytoplasm; cycle = interphase + mitosis + cytokinesis (E5-02 keeps the scope local to the w20_21 question). |
| "The chromosome splits in half"; "the chromosomes separate" at anaphase. | "The centromere divides; the sister chromatids separate and are pulled to opposite poles." "Chromosomes separate" is homologue language (meiosis, Topic 16). |
| "Nuclear membrane" used as the taught term; a global ban on it. | Teach "nuclear envelope" (syllabus wording). The R is local to w22_23 Q4(a)(iii); s23_21 Q1(a)(ii) accepted "nuclear membrane(s)" in an organelle table. |
| Cell plate in an animal cell; cleavage furrow in a plant cell. | Animal: cell surface membrane drawn in; plant: cell plate from vesicles, becoming new cell walls (E5-03). |
| "Plant cells have no spindle" (because no centrioles). | Plant cells typically lack centrioles and still form a spindle. |
| "Spindle fibres pull the chromosomes apart" at anaphase. | The spindle fibres attached to each centromere shorten, pulling the **sister chromatids** to opposite poles. |
| Telomeres "stop DNA getting shorter", "make cells immortal", "cause ageing". | The telomere is lost instead of genes; nothing about telomerase, ageing or cell death (calibration DO-NOT-ADD). |
| Stem cells "divide by differentiation"; "every stem cell division gives one stem cell and one specialised cell". | Stem cells divide by **mitosis**; daughter cells **can** differentiate; the one-stays/one-differentiates pattern is shown as typical, not universal. |
| "A tumour is cancer"; "one mutation causes cancer"; "cancer is caused by mitosis". | Benign vs malignant; typically more than one mutation; uncontrolled mitosis after mutation in genes controlling division (E5-07). |
| "Red blood cells divide to replace themselves." | Mature red blood cells have no nucleus and cannot divide; they are replaced from bone-marrow stem cells. |
| The DNA-content graph drawn as a step at S, or dropping at the wrong point for its axis. | S is a slope; per cell drops at cytokinesis; per nucleus at the end of nuclear division; read the axis label first. |
| "Most cells are in interphase because they are resting." | Interphase is active (growth, replication); most cells are seen in interphase because it is typically the longest part of the cycle, for a tissue whose cells divide independently. |
| Stage identified with two alternatives. | Commit to one stage, name the visible evidence (E5-05). |
| Root-tip method drawn impossibly: coverslip slid sideways, stain "turning" through another colour, timer started before the tips touch the acid. | Vertical squash only; toluidine blue darkens in one hue; timer starts at first contact with the acid. |
| A drawn field or a count presented as a real photomicrograph or measured data. | "schematic drawing — not a photomicrograph"; "count from the schematic field; teaching data". Real micrographs sourced and licensed, never generated. |
| "Detail is not required" read as permission to be vague in teaching. | It is Learner Guide guidance for **outline** answers (EXAMINER-INSIGHT §4); the lesson still explains fully (CONTENT-ARCHITECTURE §3). |
