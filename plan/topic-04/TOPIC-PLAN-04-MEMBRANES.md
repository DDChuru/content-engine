## Outcomes in syllabus order

### 4.1.1

**Syllabus verbatim — [p21](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=21):**

> describe the fluid mosaic model of membrane structure with reference to the hydrophobic and hydrophilic interactions that account for the formation of the phospholipid bilayer and the arrangement of proteins

**Commands / content type:** describe. **STRUCTURE / MODEL**. Budget **5:00** of the 9:00 lesson 4.1.1-2, 0 errors.

**What we teach.** A phospholipid (recalled by label from Topic 2: glycerol, two fatty-acid tails, a phosphate-containing head) has a **hydrophilic head** and **hydrophobic tails**. In water, phospholipids arrange themselves so the heads face the water on both sides and the tails face each other, away from the water: a **bilayer**. Taught as interactions, not as the tails "hating" water: the heads interact with water (hydrophilic interactions, including hydrogen bonds from water to the charged/polar head), while water molecules hydrogen-bond with each other and the non-polar tails are excluded and held together by hydrophobic interactions, so the bilayer is the arrangement in which each part is where it interacts best. Proteins sit in the bilayer according to the same logic: **intrinsic (transmembrane) proteins** have regions with hydrophobic R groups in contact with the tails and hydrophilic regions in contact with water on each side; **extrinsic (peripheral) proteins** sit on a surface, attached to the heads or to intrinsic proteins. "**Fluid**": phospholipids and many proteins move sideways within their layer; "**mosaic**": proteins scattered through the bilayer like tiles in a mosaic, in varied sizes and positions. Framing (why it exists): a cell is a watery compartment in watery surroundings, and the bilayer is the arrangement that keeps a water-based inside separate from a water-based outside while letting the cell control what crosses; "a model, introduced in 1972, that continues to be modified" (syllabus introduction) is said once.

**Real-world samples.** None handled. An explain-beat example: the membrane drawn is the cell surface membrane of a named cell (a red blood cell), so "outside" is plasma and "inside" is cytoplasm; both labelled.

**Exact mark-scheme keys:** none in the G04 sample. `UNVERIFIED — a marked 4.1.1 question (describe the fluid mosaic model) with its MS wording.` The exam close names the describe form from the syllabus command only and says no sampled question is cited.

**Scope ceiling and boundaries:** no membrane thickness figures, no freeze-fracture evidence, no lipid rafts, no flip-flop, no membrane potentials. Roles of the components are 4.1.3's.

**Motion:** self-assembly of scattered phospholipids into a bilayer (and not a micelle: the micelle is not taught) as motion in a water field; lateral drift of phospholipids and proteins; proteins never cross between layers.

**Visual needs:** `FluidMosaicMembrane` (published here), `PhospholipidToken` (published here; Topic 2 recall by label), `WaterField`.

### 4.1.3

**Syllabus verbatim — [p21](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=21):**

> describe the roles of phospholipids, cholesterol, glycolipids, proteins and glycoproteins in cell surface membranes, with reference to stability, fluidity, permeability, transport (carrier proteins and channel proteins), cell signalling (cell surface receptors) and cell recognition (cell surface antigens – see 11.1.2)

**Commands / content type:** describe. **FUNCTION / ROLES**. Budget **9:45**, 12 teaching beats, **1 error (E43)**.

**What we teach.** Each component's role, on the familiar 4.1.1-2 membrane, organised by the six named headings so no heading is starved:

- *Permeability:* the **phospholipid bilayer's hydrophobic core** is a barrier to ions and to polar (water-soluble) molecules; small non-polar molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide cross it readily. This makes the membrane **partially permeable**, and it is why ions and polar molecules such as glucose cross by protein routes.
- *Transport:* **channel proteins** provide a water-filled, hydrophilic pore through which particular ions or polar molecules pass; **carrier proteins** bind a particular molecule or ion and change shape to move it across. (How each works as a process, passive or active, is 4.2.1a/4.2.1b; here only their role.)
- *Fluidity and stability:* phospholipids moving sideways give fluidity; **cholesterol** regulates fluidity: in the typical account it reduces fluidity at higher temperatures and prevents the tails packing too tightly at low temperatures, and it helps make the membrane more stable and less permeable to small polar molecules and ions. Said with "helps" and "in animal cell membranes"; no temperature numbers. Glycolipids and glycoproteins contribute to stability by forming hydrogen bonds with surrounding water (said as "can help").
- *Cell signalling:* some glycoproteins (and proteins) act as **cell surface receptors**: a binding site with a shape complementary to a particular signalling molecule; binding leads to a response inside the cell (the stages are 4.1.4's).
- *Cell recognition:* glycoproteins and glycolipids act as **cell surface antigens**, so cells can be recognised (self / non-self is 11.1.2's; named, not taught). Real example, labelled as example: the ABO blood-group antigens on red blood cells are carbohydrate chains on glycolipids and glycoproteins (`UNVERIFIED — a syllabus or MS source for the ABO example`; if unverified at storyboard stage, it is shown as our example with the caption *example; not required*).

Framing: the membrane is not just a wall; each component gives the cell a way to control what crosses, to stay intact, and to communicate.

**Real-world samples.** None handled. Explain-beat examples: oxygen crossing a red blood cell's bilayer; glucose needing a protein route; the ABO example above.

### 4.2.1

**Syllabus verbatim — [p21](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=21):**

> describe and explain the processes of simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis, active transport, endocytosis and exocytosis

**Commands / content type:** describe and explain. **MECHANISM / PROCESS**. Budget **20:30**, 24 teaching beats, **3 errors (E45–E47)**. **Delivered as two linked lessons, split at the passive/energy-requiring boundary: 4.2.1a (simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis; 10:15, 13 teaching beats, E45) and 4.2.1b (active transport, endocytosis, exocytosis, and the comparison of facilitated diffusion with active transport; 10:15, 11 teaching beats, E46 and E47).** 4.2.1a ends on its own recap and exam close; 4.2.1b opens on a labelled recall of 4.2.1a's passive processes and the `FluidMosaicMembrane`, then stands alone.

**What we teach — 4.2.1a (passive).** One explanation grammar: particles move randomly because of their kinetic energy; where there is a **concentration gradient**, more particles move from the higher to the lower concentration than the reverse, so there is a **net movement** down the gradient until concentrations are equal (then movement continues both ways, with no net movement). Passive: no energy from ATP is used; the kinetic energy of the particles drives it.

- *Simple diffusion* across a membrane: small, non-polar molecules (O₂, CO₂) pass directly through the phospholipid bilayer, down their concentration gradient. Real-world explain example: oxygen diffusing from alveolar air into a red blood cell (Topic 9 context named, not taught).
- *Facilitated diffusion*: ions and polar molecules (e.g. glucose) cannot cross the hydrophobic core readily, so they diffuse through **channel proteins** (a hydrophilic pore) or are moved by **carrier proteins** (binding and a change of shape), still **down** their concentration gradient, still passive. Channel and carrier are kept as two mechanisms; a channel is never given a carrier's shape-change mechanism (G04's own check).
- *Osmosis*: stated in water-potential terms from the start: **osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential, through a partially permeable membrane**. Water potential introduced as the tendency of water to move; pure water has the highest water potential, set at **zero**; dissolving solute lowers the water potential, so solutions have **negative** water potentials; "higher" means less negative. The phrase "concentration of water" is not used (trap table). Water molecules cross in both directions; the net movement is towards the lower water potential. Water crosses the bilayer and, in many cells, channel proteins (aquaporins: named as "in many cells", not taught).
- *Factors on the rate of diffusion*, qualitatively: steeper gradient, larger surface area, shorter distance, higher temperature (more kinetic energy) → faster; small and non-polar molecules cross the bilayer faster. No equation. The surface-area and distance points are handed forward to 4.2.3-4 by label.

Framing: cells must exchange substances with their surroundings all the time, and passive processes cost the cell no ATP; the membrane decides which substances can take which route. Hook (VIDEO-STRUCTURE's own): *Ever wondered how sugar gets into a cell, when the membrane is built to keep water-loving things out?*, answered by facilitated diffusion. Handle: *a door in a wall* for a channel protein, converted at once into *a channel protein provides a hydrophilic pore through which particular ions or polar molecules diffuse down their concentration gradient* (the "revolving door" for a carrier, VIDEO-STRUCTURE's own example, belongs to 4.2.1b's carrier states; each lesson uses only its own handle).

**What we teach — 4.2.1b (energy-requiring and bulk).**

- *Active transport*: movement of molecules or ions across a membrane **against** their concentration gradient (from lower to higher concentration), through **carrier proteins**, using **energy from ATP** made in respiration. Mechanism as motion: the ion binds the carrier's binding site on one side; ATP is hydrolysed (token switch, caption above); the carrier changes shape; the ion is released on the other side; the carrier returns to its original shape. Real-world explain example: root hair cells taking up mineral ions such as nitrate from soil water, where the concentration is lower than inside the cell (Topic 7 context named; the plan does not specify the carrier).
- *Endocytosis*: the cell surface membrane surrounds material and pinches off to form a **vesicle** inside the cell, using energy from ATP. **Phagocytosis**: solid material, such as a bacterium engulfed by a phagocyte (named; immunity is Topic 11's). **Pinocytosis**: liquid, with dissolved substances, taken in small vesicles.
- *Exocytosis*: a vesicle moves to the cell surface membrane and fuses with it, releasing its contents outside, using energy from ATP. Real-world example: digestive enzymes released from pancreatic cells (recall 3.1.1's extracellular enzymes by label), and 4.1.4's ligand secretion.
- *Comparison, facilitated diffusion versus active transport* (the m24_22 Q1(b)(ii) form): similarity: both move specific substances through transport proteins, carrier proteins in both cases for this comparison (facilitated diffusion may also use channels); differences: direction relative to the concentration gradient (down vs against) and energy (no ATP vs ATP from respiration).
- *Saturation* (the s23_21 Q3(b)(i) form): on a graph of rate of uptake against external concentration, simple diffusion keeps rising, while carrier-mediated uptake (facilitated or active) levels off because **the number of carrier proteins in the membrane is limited**; when all are occupied, more substrate outside cannot increase the rate. The protein is a carrier protein with a **binding site**, not an enzyme with an active site (same vocabulary guard as E44).

Handle for 4.2.1b: *a revolving door* for a carrier protein (VIDEO-STRUCTURE's own example), converted at once: *a carrier protein binds a specific molecule or ion and changes shape to move it across the membrane*; for active transport, *energy from ATP drives that change of shape against the gradient*.

**Real-world samples (4.2.1a/b).** None handled. Explain-beat examples as named above (alveolar oxygen; glucose into a cell; root hair nitrate uptake; phagocyte and bacterium; pancreatic enzyme secretion).

**Exact mark-scheme keys and examiner credit (PDF-UNCHECKED):** [m24_22 Q1(b)(i), 1 mark, MS PDF p.5]; [s23_21 Q3(a), 1 mark, MS PDF p.11]; [m24_22 Q1(b)(ii), 3 marks, MS PDF p.6]: compare facilitated diffusion with active transport, giving the required similarity and differences (G04 paraphrase; `UNVERIFIED — MS wording`); [s23_21 Q3(b)(i), 2 marks, MS PDF p.11]: interpret uptake saturation as a limited number of transport proteins (G04 paraphrase; `UNVERIFIED — MS wording`); [w22_23 Q2(a)(i), MS PDF p.9] R “active transport” for engulfing bacteria; [s23_21 Q1(b)(i), MS PDF p.8] R “phagocytosis”, with endocytosis/pinocytosis credited for the depicted uptake (G04's description of the credit; `UNVERIFIED — what the s23_21 Q1(b)(i) diagram depicts`). **Syllabus verbatim — [p22](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=22):**

> explain the movement of water between cells and solutions in terms of water potential and explain the different effects of the movement of water on plant cells and animal cells (knowledge of solute potential and pressure potential is not expected)

**Commands / content type:** explain. **MECHANISM / COMPARISON**. Budget **8:15**, 11 teaching beats, 0 errors.

**What we teach.** Using the `WaterPotentialModel` from 4.2.1a (recalled by label; this lesson stands alone and re-states the definition): water moves by osmosis from the higher (less negative) to the lower (more negative) water potential. Three cases for each cell type, always stated as a comparison of the **solution's** water potential with the **cell's**:

- *Animal cell (red blood cell)*: solution with a higher water potential than the cell → net water entry → the cell swells and may burst (**haemolysis**: named), because there is no cell wall to resist expansion; equal water potentials → no net movement; solution with a lower water potential → net water loss → the cell shrinks (**crenated**: named as the appearance).
- *Plant cell*: higher water potential outside → net water entry → the vacuole and cytoplasm swell and the protoplast pushes against the cell wall; the cell becomes **turgid**; the wall resists further expansion, so the cell does not burst; lower water potential outside → net water loss → the protoplast shrinks, the cell becomes **flaccid**, and with further loss the cell surface membrane pulls away from the wall: **plasmolysis**; the wall is freely permeable, so the space between wall and membrane fills with the external solution.
- The difference in outcome between the two cell types comes from the **cell wall** (the SME AL 4.2.12 ceiling's emphasis, and the syllabus's "different effects").

Explained without ψs/ψp: "the wall resists expansion" is said; "pressure potential" is never named. ## Known traps to hand the checker

Perform the sentence-level sweep for **all, every, always, only, never, cannot, no, because** on every storyboard. These are author checks, not additional error beats.

| Tempting over-claim or slip | Required treatment |
|---|---|
| Osmosis defined as water moving "from a high concentration of water to a low concentration of water", or "from dilute to concentrated" as the definition. | Osmosis in water-potential terms: net movement of water molecules from higher to lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane. "Concentration of water" is never used; "dilute/concentrated solution" may describe a solution but not define osmosis. |
| Diffusion, facilitated diffusion and active transport blurred: "facilitated diffusion uses energy", "active transport moves substances down a gradient", "channels use ATP". | Diffusion and facilitated diffusion are passive and down the gradient; facilitated diffusion uses channel or carrier proteins; active transport uses carrier proteins and energy from ATP to move substances against the gradient. |
| A channel protein given a carrier's shape-change mechanism. | Channel: a hydrophilic pore; carrier: binding and change of shape (G04's check). |
| "Ions cannot pass through the membrane." | Ions cannot cross the bilayer's hydrophobic core; they cross through channel or carrier proteins (E43; the MS ignore line is question-local). |
| "Glucose is too big to cross the membrane." | Glucose is polar, so it cannot cross the hydrophobic core readily; it crosses by facilitated diffusion (E45). Size matters for some molecules; the credited reason here is polarity/the hydrophobic interior. |
| "The receptor's active site." | Binding site; "active site" belongs to enzymes (E44). |
| Carrier saturation explained as "no more diffusion" or "active sites full". | The number of carrier proteins is limited; all occupied; binding site (E47). |
| Engulfing called "active transport"; every vesicle uptake called "phagocytosis". | Endocytosis: phagocytosis (solids), pinocytosis (liquids); bulk transport is not carrier-mediated active transport (E46). Phagocytosis stays correct for a phagocyte engulfing a bacterium. |
| "Phospholipids are hydrophobic"; heads drawn in the core. | Hydrophilic heads, hydrophobic tails; orientation fixed in every frame. |
| Glycolipids/glycoproteins' carbohydrate chains drawn on both faces or in the cytoplasm. | External face only. |
| Cholesterol "makes the membrane rigid" as its whole role; "found in all membranes". | Regulates fluidity (less fluid at higher temperature, prevents tight packing at low temperature), helps stability, reduces permeability to small polar molecules and ions; "in animal cell membranes". |
| Membrane components' movement animated as flip-flop, or proteins crossing leaflets. | Lateral drift only. |
| "Every cell responds to a hormone in the blood." | Only cells with complementary receptors respond; the ligand reaches many cells. |
| A cell-signalling lesson that becomes a cascade lesson. | Three stages; "a response inside the target cell"; no second messengers (calibration DO-NOT-ADD). |
| "Plant cells burst in pure water." | The cell wall resists expansion; the cell becomes turgid; animal cells (red blood cells) can burst (haemolysis). |
| "Plasmolysis" used for animal cells; "the plasmolysed space is empty/air". | Plasmolysis is the membrane pulling away from the wall in plant cells; the gap fills with external solution; animal cells shrink (crenation). |
| "Water potential of the solution is −860 kPa" written without context; "higher water potential = more negative". | Higher water potential is less negative; pure water 0; the −860 kPa is w20_51's result only. |
| ψs / ψp named or calculated. | Not expected (4.2.6); never named. |
| "Visking tubing is a model of the cell membrane." | Visking is partially permeable by pore size only; no bilayer, no proteins; glucose crosses Visking by pores but needs a transport protein across a living membrane. |
| "Sucrose cannot pass through Visking tubing." | Visking's pores also pass sucrose, more slowly than water; the osmometer rise shows net water entry over the run (REAL-WORLD fit statement). |
| "The colourless zone in the agar cube is where the acid is." | It is where enough acid has neutralised the alkali to take the pH below the indicator's change; the acid front leads slightly. |
| "Bigger organisms have less surface area." | They have more surface area but less surface area per unit volume (a smaller SA:V). |
| Time to decolourise called "the rate of diffusion". | An endpoint time; 1/t is a relative rate proxy for comparing cube sizes. |
| The zero-crossing concentration drawn as a measured point; the lookup value treated as a reading. | Construction lines; the lookup is supplied data; the estimate is labelled an estimate. |
| "The same amount of potato/solution." | Name the quantity, unit and operation: cylinders of stated length and diameter, mass to 0.01 g; 20.0 cm³ of solution (E48; the "not specific" key message is paper-wide). |
| "Heat forces the beetroot pigment out." | Heat damages the membranes (proteins denatured, bilayer disrupted), so the pigment can diffuse out. |
| A beetroot result generalised to "membranes break at 70 °C". | This tissue, this time, these conditions; one comparison. |
| Real-world extras presented as marking points in exam-question beats. | Mark-scheme answer first; extras spoken as beyond the scheme and shown on a panel labelled "beyond the mark scheme". |
