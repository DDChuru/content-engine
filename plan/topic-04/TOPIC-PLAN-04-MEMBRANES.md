## Outcomes in syllabus order

### 4.1.1

**Syllabus verbatim — [p21](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=21):**

> describe the fluid mosaic model of membrane structure with reference to the hydrophobic and hydrophilic interactions that account for the formation of the phospholipid bilayer and the arrangement of proteins

**Commands / content type:** describe. **STRUCTURE / MODEL**. Budget **5:00** of the 9:00 lesson 4.1.1-2, 0 errors.

**What we teach.** A phospholipid (recalled by label from Topic 2: glycerol, two fatty-acid tails, a phosphate-containing head) has a **hydrophilic head** and **hydrophobic tails**. In water, phospholipids arrange themselves so the heads face the water on both sides and the tails face each other, away from the water: a **bilayer**. Taught as interactions, not as the tails "hating" water: the heads interact with water (hydrophilic interactions, including hydrogen bonds between water and the charged/polar head), while water molecules hydrogen-bond with each other and the non-polar tails are excluded and held together by hydrophobic interactions, so the bilayer is the arrangement in which each part is where it interacts best. Proteins sit in the bilayer according to the same logic: **intrinsic proteins are embedded in the bilayer; the channel, carrier and receptor examples drawn here span it and are transmembrane proteins** (should-fix 1), with regions of hydrophobic R groups in contact with the tails and hydrophilic regions in contact with water; **extrinsic (peripheral) proteins** sit on a surface, attached to the heads or to intrinsic proteins. "**Fluid**": phospholipids and many proteins move sideways within their layer; "**mosaic**": proteins scattered through the bilayer like tiles in a mosaic, in varied sizes and positions. Framing (why it exists): a cell is a watery compartment in watery surroundings, and the bilayer is the arrangement that keeps a water-based inside separate from a water-based outside while letting the cell control what crosses; "a model, introduced in 1972, that continues to be modified" (syllabus introduction) is said once. Hook: a real question about why a cell does not simply dissolve into or mix with the water around it (worded by the author). Handle: to be chosen by the author and converted at once into the creditworthy sentence; no invented metaphor that could be written in an exam.

**Real-world samples.** None handled. An explain-beat example: the membrane drawn is the cell surface membrane of a named cell (a red blood cell), so "outside" is plasma and "inside" is cytoplasm; both labelled.

**Exact mark-scheme keys:** none for 4.1.1 in the cited question blocks (plan check: 4.1.1 0/5). The 4.1.1-2 exam close uses M24/22 Q1(a)(ii) (see 4.1.2); it validates the 4.1.2 portion and is not a full fluid-mosaic-model question.

**Scope ceiling and boundaries:** no membrane thickness figures, no freeze-fracture evidence, no lipid rafts, no flip-flop, no membrane potentials. Roles of the components are 4.1.3's.

**Motion:** self-assembly of scattered phospholipids into a bilayer (a micelle is not taught) as motion in a water field; lateral drift of phospholipids and proteins; proteins never cross between layers.

**Visual needs:** `FluidMosaicMembrane` (published here), `PhospholipidToken` (published here; Topic 2 recall by label), `WaterField`.

**Recorded examiner evidence / supported error treatment:** none selected; the absence of an allocated beat is not an absence of evidence.

### 4.1.2

**Syllabus verbatim — [p21](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=21):**

> describe the arrangement of cholesterol, glycolipids and glycoproteins in cell surface membranes

**Commands / content type:** describe. **STRUCTURE / ARRANGEMENT**. Budget **4:00** of the 9:00 lesson 4.1.1-2, 0 errors.

**What we teach.** Placed on the same membrane, one at a time: **cholesterol** molecules sit among the phospholipids in both layers of animal cell surface membranes, their small hydrophilic OH end level with the phospholipid heads (polar end towards the watery heads) and their rigid, non-polar ring structure among the tails — the arrangement follows the same polar/non-polar interactions as the phospholipids (said as "in animal cell membranes"; not claimed for every membrane type). **Glycolipids**: lipids with a carbohydrate chain attached, the lipid part in the outer layer, the carbohydrate chain projecting from the **external** surface. **Glycoproteins**: proteins with carbohydrate chains attached, the chains projecting from the external surface. The carbohydrate chains face outwards into the watery surroundings (they are hydrophilic), not into the bilayer and not into the cytoplasm. Arrangement only; what each does is 4.1.3.

### 4.1.3

**Syllabus verbatim — [p21](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=21):**

> describe the roles of phospholipids, cholesterol, glycolipids, proteins and glycoproteins in cell surface membranes, with reference to stability, fluidity, permeability, transport (carrier proteins and channel proteins), cell signalling (cell surface receptors) and cell recognition (cell surface antigens – see 11.1.2)

**Commands / content type:** describe. **FUNCTION / ROLES**. Budget **10:15** (teaching 9:00 + one complete error beat 1:15), 12 teaching beats, **1 error (E43)**.

**What we teach.** Each component's role, on the familiar 4.1.1-2 membrane, organised by the six named headings so no heading is starved:

- *Permeability:* the **phospholipid bilayer's hydrophobic core** is a barrier to ions and to polar (water-soluble) molecules; small non-polar or lipid-soluble molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide (and, in S21/22 Q3(b)'s context, a steroid hormone) cross it readily. This makes the membrane **partially permeable**. **Glucose is polar and does not cross the hydrophobic bilayer core readily; it needs a transport protein** (MF2 wording; the direction and energy of any particular glucose system are 4.2.1's). Sodium ions are charged and likewise need a protein route (the M24/22 Q1(b)(i) context).
- *Transport:* **channel proteins** provide a water-filled, hydrophilic pore through which particular ions or polar molecules pass (not every channel has the same selectivity: said as "particular"); **carrier proteins** bind a particular molecule or ion and change shape to move it across. (How each works as a process, passive or active, is 4.2.1a/4.2.1b; here only their role.)
- *Fluidity and stability:* phospholipids moving sideways give fluidity; **cholesterol** regulates fluidity: in the typical account it reduces fluidity at higher temperatures and prevents the tails packing too tightly at low temperatures, and it helps make the membrane more stable and less permeable to small polar molecules and ions (M24/22 Q1(a)(iii) asks for one role of cholesterol). Said with "helps" and "in animal cell membranes"; no temperature numbers. Glycolipids and glycoproteins can help stability by forming hydrogen bonds with surrounding water.
- *Cell signalling:* **some** glycoproteins (and proteins) act as **cell surface receptors**: a binding site with a shape complementary to a particular signalling molecule; binding leads to a response in the cell (the stages are 4.1.4's). Not every glycoprotein is a receptor.
- *Cell recognition:* glycoproteins and glycolipids act as **cell surface antigens**, so cells can be recognised (self / non-self is 11.1.2's; named, not taught). **The ABO example is retained only with a verified content source at storyboard stage** (plan check); otherwise it is omitted. Not every carbohydrate chain is an ABO antigen.

Framing: the membrane is not just a wall; each component gives the cell a way to control what crosses, to stay intact, and to communicate.

### 4.2.1

**Syllabus verbatim — [p21](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=21):**

> describe and explain the processes of simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis, active transport, endocytosis and exocytosis

**Commands / content type:** describe and explain. **MECHANISM / PROCESS**. Budget **22:00**, 24 teaching beats, **3 errors (E45–E47)**. **Delivered as two linked lessons, split at the passive/energy-requiring boundary: 4.2.1a (simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis; 10:45, 13 teaching beats, E45) and 4.2.1b (active transport, endocytosis, exocytosis, and the comparison of facilitated diffusion with active transport; 11:15, 11 teaching beats, E46 and E47).** 4.2.1a ends on its own recap and exam close; 4.2.1b opens on a labelled recall of 4.2.1a's passive processes and the `FluidMosaicMembrane`, then stands alone.

**What we teach — 4.2.1a (passive).** One explanation grammar: particles move randomly because of their kinetic energy; where there is a **concentration gradient**, more particles move from the higher to the lower concentration than the reverse, so there is a **net movement** down the gradient until concentrations are equal (then movement continues both ways, with no net movement). Passive: no energy from ATP is used; the kinetic energy of the particles drives it.

- *Simple diffusion* across a membrane: small, non-polar or lipid-soluble molecules (O₂, CO₂) pass directly through the phospholipid bilayer, down their concentration gradient. Real-world explain example: oxygen diffusing from alveolar air into a red blood cell (Topic 9 context named, not taught). S21/22 Q3(b)'s steroid hormone crossing the bilayer is an exam context for the same property (non-polar/lipid-soluble).
- *Facilitated diffusion*: ions and polar molecules cannot cross the hydrophobic core readily, so they diffuse through **channel proteins** (a hydrophilic pore) or are moved by **carrier proteins** (binding and a change of shape), still **down** their concentration gradient, still passive. Channel and carrier are kept as two mechanisms; a channel is never given a carrier's shape-change mechanism. **Glucose (MF2, exact):** "Glucose is polar and does not cross the hydrophobic bilayer core readily; it needs a transport protein. In our facilitated-diffusion example, a carrier moves glucose down its concentration gradient without ATP. The protein requirement alone does not establish the direction or energy requirement of every glucose-transport system. In S23/21 Q3(a), naming facilitated diffusion is not the credited reason; state polarity and the hydrophobic bilayer first."
- *Osmosis*: **MF3 replacement, exact:** "Water potential describes water's tendency to move. Pure water at atmospheric pressure is the reference, with water potential 0 kPa. At the same temperature and pressure, adding solute lowers water potential, so the solutions in this comparison have negative values; a less negative value is higher. Net osmosis is from higher to lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane. Water still crosses both ways when the water potentials are equal." Definition as taught: **osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential, through a partially permeable membrane**. The phrase "concentration of water" is not used. Water crosses the bilayer and, in many cells, channel proteins (aquaporins: named as "in many cells", not taught). Higher/lower comparisons are labelled as the **initial condition**.
- *Factors on the rate of diffusion*, qualitatively: steeper gradient, larger surface area, shorter distance, higher temperature (more kinetic energy) → faster; small and non-polar molecules cross the bilayer faster. No equation. Surface area and distance are handed forward to 4.2.3-4 by label.

Handle: *a door in a wall* for a channel protein, converted at once into *a channel protein provides a hydrophilic pore through which particular ions or polar molecules diffuse down their concentration gradient*.

**What we teach — 4.2.1b (energy-requiring and bulk).**

- *Active transport*: movement of molecules or ions across a membrane **against** their concentration gradient (from lower to higher concentration), through **carrier proteins**, using **energy from ATP** made in respiration. Mechanism as motion: the ion binds the carrier's binding site on one side; ATP is hydrolysed (token switch, caption above); the carrier changes shape; the ion is released on the other side; the carrier returns to its original shape. **MF4 model guard, exact:** "The ATP-hydrolysis animation is a simplified directly ATP-driven carrier model. Keep its transported ion generic. Root mineral uptake illustrates energy-dependent accumulation, but do not label the animated ATP-hydrolysing carrier as a nitrate transporter; the example does not specify that molecular mechanism. Co-transport is not taught here." Real-world explain example: root hair cells accumulating mineral ions from soil water against a concentration gradient, using energy (the animated carrier stays generic).
- *Endocytosis*: the cell surface membrane surrounds material and pinches off to form a **vesicle** inside the cell, using energy from ATP. **Phagocytosis**: solid material, such as bacteria engulfed by a macrophage (the W22/23 Q2(a)(i) context; immunity is Topic 11's). **Pinocytosis**: liquid, with dissolved substances, taken in small vesicles (the S23/21 Q1(b)(i) context: inward budding at X from a plant cell surface membrane during vacuole development).
- *Exocytosis*: a vesicle moves to the cell surface membrane and fuses with it, releasing its contents outside, using energy from ATP. Examples: release of pectin from plant cells (the S21/22 Q5(b)(ii) context); digestive enzymes released from pancreatic cells (recall 3.1.1's extracellular enzymes by label); 4.1.4's insulin secretion.
- *Comparison, facilitated diffusion versus active transport* (the M24/22 Q1(b)(ii) form: one similarity and two differences, 3 marks): similarity: both move specific substances through transport proteins; **a conformational-change similarity applies only when the facilitated diffusion uses a carrier** (the scheme rejects the incorrect channel context: plan check); differences: direction relative to the concentration gradient (down vs against) and energy (no ATP vs ATP from respiration).
- *Saturation* (the S23/21 Q3(b)(i) form: grape hexose transporters in mutant yeast; plateau/constant uptake and carriers at capacity, 2 marks): on a graph of rate of uptake against external concentration, simple diffusion keeps rising, while carrier-mediated uptake levels off because **the number of carrier proteins in the membrane is limited**; when all are occupied, more substrate outside cannot increase the rate. The protein is a carrier protein with a **binding site**, not an enzyme with an active site. **Saturation alone is not a universal way to distinguish active from facilitated transport** (plan check).

Handle for 4.2.1b: *a revolving door* for a carrier protein (VIDEO-STRUCTURE's own example), converted at once: *a carrier protein binds a specific molecule or ion and changes shape to move it across the membrane*; for active transport, *energy from ATP drives that change of shape against the gradient*.

### 4.2.6

**Syllabus verbatim — [p22](syllabus/664560-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf#page=22):**

> explain the movement of water between cells and solutions in terms of water potential and explain the different effects of the movement of water on plant cells and animal cells (knowledge of solute potential and pressure potential is not expected)

**Commands / content type:** explain. **MECHANISM / COMPARISON**. Budget **8:15**, 11 teaching beats, 0 errors.

**What we teach.** Using the `WaterPotentialModel` from 4.2.1a (recalled by label; this lesson stands alone and re-states the MF3 water-potential sentences and the definition): net osmosis is from the higher (less negative) to the lower (more negative) water potential. Three cases for **each** cell type, always stated as a comparison of the **solution's** water potential with the **cell's**, labelled as the **initial condition**:

- *Animal cell (red blood cell)*: solution with a higher water potential than the cell → net water entry → the cell swells and may burst (**haemolysis**: the red-blood-cell term; other animal cells may lyse without that name), because there is no cell wall to resist expansion; **equal water potentials → no net movement: the red blood cell remains unchanged in this particular comparison** (equality by itself does not determine a cell's shape); solution with a lower water potential → net water loss → the cell shrinks (**crenated**: named as the appearance).
- *Plant cell*: higher water potential outside → net water entry → the vacuole and cytoplasm swell and the protoplast pushes against the cell wall; the cell becomes **turgid**; the wall resists further expansion, so the cell does not burst. **Equal water potentials → no net movement.** Lower water potential outside → net water loss → the protoplast shrinks, the cell becomes **flaccid**, and with further loss the cell surface membrane pulls away from the wall: **plasmolysis**; the wall is freely permeable, so the space between wall and membrane fills with the external solution.
- **Equilibrium (MF3, exact):** "Label higher/lower comparisons as the initial condition. As a plant cell takes up water, its expanding contents press against the resisting wall; its water potential rises until it equals that of the surrounding solution and there is no net water entry. Show the net arrow fading at equilibrium while individual water molecules continue crossing both ways. A turgid plant cell can therefore have the same water potential as its surroundings. Equal water potentials mean no net movement, not necessarily a flaccid cell or equal solute concentrations. Similarly, show a normal red blood cell remaining unchanged in the particular equal-potential comparison; do not teach that equality by itself determines a cell's shape. No component-potential names or equations are introduced."
- The difference in outcome between the two cell types comes from the **cell wall**.

Explained without ψs/ψp: "the wall resists expansion" and "the cell's water potential rises" are said; the component potentials are never named. Framing: cells must keep their water content in a working range; plants use turgor for support (a wilting lettuce crisping in water: a turgor demonstration, not a water-potential measurement); animals keep the water potential around their cells controlled — **"An isotonic saline example shows why matching the effective osmotic conditions around red blood cells can prevent large net changes in their volume."** (MF6 replacement for the draft's drip claim; no prescription, universal IV-fluid claim or clinical digression).

**Real-world samples.** Blood cells are shown as a model, not handled; if a micrograph of red blood cells in solutions is used, it is **sourced**, never generated (asset dependency). Lettuce: what it shows (net water entry restores turgidity) and where it stops (it does not measure water potential).
