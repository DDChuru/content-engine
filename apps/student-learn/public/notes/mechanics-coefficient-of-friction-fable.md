# Coefficient of Friction

Friction is the force between a surface and an object that resists the object sliding across the surface. It acts **parallel to the surface**, in the direction **opposite to the way the object would slide**.

## When does friction act?

- A crate resting on a rough horizontal floor with only its weight \(mg\) and the normal reaction \(R\) acting: **no friction**. Nothing is trying to slide it.
- Add a horizontal pull: friction appears, opposing the pull, and it grows to match the pull while the crate stays at rest.
- Friction has a limit. Past the limit the crate slides, and friction stays at its maximum while the extra pull accelerates the crate.
- On a **smooth** surface \(\mu = 0\), so there is no friction however hard you pull. Only a **rough** surface gives friction.

## The rule

\[
f \le \mu R
\]

Here \(f\) is the friction actually acting, \(R\) the normal reaction, and \(\mu\) the **coefficient of friction**, a property of the two surfaces in contact (large for rubber on gravel, small for wet ice). The largest friction possible is

\[
f_{\max} = \mu R .
\]

When \(f = f_{\max}\) and the object is still at rest it is in **limiting equilibrium**: on the point of moving. Friction is only at its maximum when the object is about to move or already moving. Otherwise it is just whatever balances the other horizontal forces.

## Worked example: three trials

A crate of mass \(5\text{ kg}\) rests on a rough horizontal floor with \(\mu = 0.4\); take \(g = 10\text{ m s}^{-2}\). A horizontal rope pulls it with force \(X\). Three separate trials, each from rest: \(X = 12\text{ N}\), \(20\text{ N}\), \(25\text{ N}\). Find the friction \(f\) and the acceleration \(a\) each time.

### Normal reaction and the limit

Vertically the crate does not move:
\[
R - mg = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad R = 5 \times 10 = 50\text{ N}.
\]
\[
f_{\max} = \mu R = 0.4 \times 50 = 20\text{ N}.
\]
Every trial is compared with this 20 N.

### Trial 1: \(X = 12\text{ N}\)

\(12 < 20\), so the crate stays at rest and friction matches the pull. Horizontally, resultant force \(= ma\):
\[
X - f = ma \quad\Rightarrow\quad 12 - f = 5 \times 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{f = 12\text{ N},\ a = 0}.
\]
Friction is 12 N, not 20 N: it only supplies what is needed.

### Trial 2: \(X = 20\text{ N}\)

\(20 = f_{\max}\): **limiting equilibrium**, still at rest but on the point of moving.
\[
20 - f = 5 \times 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{f = 20\text{ N},\ a = 0}.
\]

### Trial 3: \(X = 25\text{ N}\)

\(25 > 20\), so the crate slides and friction is at its maximum, \(f = 20\text{ N}\).
\[
X - f = ma \quad\Rightarrow\quad 25 - 20 = 5a \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{a = 1\text{ m s}^{-2}} \text{ to the right}.
\]
Only the pull beyond the limit accelerates the crate.

## What would change on ice?

With \(\mu = 0.1\): \(f_{\max} = 0.1 \times 50 = 5\text{ N}\). Now even the 12 N pull exceeds the limit, so the crate slides in trial 1 as well.

## Remember

- Say when friction acts, and which way: parallel to the surface, opposite to the impending or actual sliding; none when nothing pushes sideways.
- Use \(f \le \mu R\) and \(f_{\max} = \mu R\).
- Decide: compare the pull with \(f_{\max}\): at rest (\(f\) = pull, \(a = 0\)), limiting equilibrium (\(f = f_{\max}\), \(a = 0\)), or sliding (\(f = f_{\max}\), \(a > 0\) from \(F = ma\)).
