# Equilibrium in 1D — five-minute revision

## Resultant force and equilibrium

The resultant force is the vector sum of all forces acting on a particle. Along one straight line, choose a positive direction and add the forces algebraically: forces in the opposite direction carry a minus sign.

A particle is in equilibrium when its resultant force is zero:

\(\sum F=0\)

This is the Cambridge 9709 syllabus condition. It does not mean that no forces act, and it does not require the same number of forces in each direction. Several forces can combine to balance one force.

By Newton’s first law, zero resultant means zero acceleration. The particle may be at rest, or it may move with constant non-zero velocity.

## Sign convention and one-line method

1. Isolate the particle and draw every force acting on it.
2. Choose one direction as positive and mark it on the diagram.
3. Give forces in the positive direction a plus sign and forces in the opposite direction a minus sign.
4. Write one equilibrium equation, \(\sum F=0\), and solve it.
5. Check that the result is physically sensible and include units.

For example, if right is positive and forces \(P\) and \(Q\) act right while \(R\) acts left, equilibrium gives

\(P+Q-R=0\)

Equivalently,

\(P+Q=R\)

Both forms are valid. The sign convention makes the first form safer when directions or unknowns are less obvious.

## Weight and \(g\)

Mass and weight are different:

\(W=mg\)

Mass is measured in kilograms; weight is a downward force measured in newtons. For Cambridge 9709 Mechanics, use

\(g=10\text{ m s}^{-2}\)

unless the question explicitly gives another value. Therefore a particle of mass \(m\text{ kg}\) normally has weight

\(W=10m\text{ N}\)

Do not multiply a force already given in newtons by \(g\).

## Worked example 1: video example

An object is in equilibrium. The upward forces are \(10\text{ N}\) and \((18+x)\text{ N}\); the downward force is \(5x\text{ N}\). Find \(x\) and the magnitude of the resultant force.

Take upward as positive:

\(10+(18+x)-5x=0\)

So

\(10+(18+x)=5x\)

\(28+x=5x\)

\(28=4x\)

\(x=7\)

Check: the upward total is \(10+25=35\text{ N}\), and the downward total is \(5(7)=35\text{ N}\). Hence the resultant has magnitude

\(0\text{ N}\)

No value of \(g\) is needed because every force is already given in newtons.

## Worked example 2: shop sign from the PDF

A sign of mass \((4x-8)\text{ kg}\) is held in equilibrium by upward tensions \((20x+9)\text{ N}\) and \((10x+11)\text{ N}\). Find \(x\), using \(g=10\text{ m s}^{-2}\).

Its downward weight is

\((4x-8)g=10(4x-8)\text{ N}\)

Take upward as positive:

\((20x+9)+(10x+11)-10(4x-8)=0\)

\(30x+20=40x-80\)

\(10x=100\)

\(x=10\)

Check: the mass is \(32\text{ kg}\), so the weight is \(320\text{ N}\). The tensions total \(209+111=320\text{ N}\), so the resultant is zero.

## Self-check

A \(6\text{ kg}\) particle is in vertical equilibrium. An upward force of \(18\text{ N}\) and an upward tension \(T\text{ N}\) act on it. Find \(T\), using \(g=10\text{ m s}^{-2}\).

### Answer

The weight is \(6g=60\text{ N}\) downward. Taking upward as positive,

\(T+18-60=0\)

so

\(T=42\text{ N}\)

Check: \(42+18=60\), so the resultant is zero.

## Common exam slips

- Adding force magnitudes without accounting for their directions.
- Changing the positive direction part-way through the calculation.
- Writing \(\sum F=ma\) with a non-zero acceleration for a particle stated to be in equilibrium. Here \(a=0\), so \(\sum F=0\).
- Assuming equilibrium means the particle must be at rest. Constant velocity is also possible.
- Assuming there must be one force in each direction. It is the totals that balance, not the number of arrows.
- Using mass in kilograms as though it were a force in newtons.
- Forgetting to replace a mass by its weight \(mg\) when the downward force is not already given.
- Multiplying a force already stated in newtons by \(g\).
- Using \(g=9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}\) when the Cambridge 9709 question expects the normal value \(g=10\text{ m s}^{-2}\), unless told otherwise.
- Omitting the unit \(\text{N}\) from a force answer.
