# Multiple collisions — five-minute revision

## What a multiple-collision question is

A multiple-collision question follows particles through two or more direct collisions in sequence. A particle may collide with a third particle, or two particles may meet again after one of them has had another collision. The answer from one collision becomes the input to the next.

Momentum is directional. For a particle of mass \(m\) and signed velocity \(v\),

\(p=mv\)

and its unit is \(\text{kg m s}^{-1}\). Choose one positive direction for the whole question. A velocity opposite to it is negative even when the question gives a positive speed.

Cambridge 9709 includes direct collisions and particles that join. If two particles coalesce, use their common velocity immediately afterwards and treat their combined mass as one body in any later collision. Use conservation of momentum and the information supplied; do not invent an extra impact law.

## The method

1. Put the particles in their correct left-to-right order and choose a positive direction.
2. Make a short event list: collision 1, collision 2, then the possible later collision.
3. Draw a separate before-and-after diagram for each collision. Show masses, arrows and signed velocities.
4. Label each new unknown uniquely. For example, use \(v_B\) for \(B\)'s velocity after collision 1 and \(w_B\) for its velocity after collision 2.
5. Apply conservation of momentum to the two particles involved in that collision:

   \(m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2\)

6. Carry each calculated velocity, including its sign, into the next diagram. Do not reuse the particle's original velocity.
7. After the stated collisions, compare the remaining particles' positions, directions and velocities to decide whether their separation is decreasing.

Momentum is conserved for the colliding system when no external force changes its total momentum during the short collision. It is not conserved for one particle by itself. For a collision with a fixed wall, use the rebound information supplied by the question; the wall exerts an external force on the particle.

## Will there be a subsequent collision?

Suppose \(A\) is to the left of \(B\) after the relevant collisions, and right is positive. Their separation is \(x_B-x_A\), so it decreases precisely when

\(v_A>v_B\)

This one signed-velocity test covers every case:

- If they move towards one another, they will collide.
- If they move away from one another, they will not collide.
- If both move right, the rear particle \(A\) must be faster: \(v_A>v_B\).
- If both move left, the right-hand particle \(B\) is behind in the direction of travel and must have the greater speed. With signed velocities, that still gives \(v_A>v_B\); for example, \(-1>-3\).
- If one is stationary, the other must be moving towards it.
- If \(v_A=v_B\), their separation stays constant, so there is no later collision unless they are already in contact.

Always make the comparison only after both particles have reached the states being compared. Position matters as much as speed: the faster particle must be behind in its direction of travel.

## Worked example

Three spheres \(A,B,C\), in that left-to-right order, have masses \(1\text{ kg}\), \(2\text{ kg}\) and \(3\text{ kg}\). They initially move right at \(4\text{ m s}^{-1}\), \(3\text{ m s}^{-1}\) and \(1\text{ m s}^{-1}\), respectively. After \(A\) collides with \(B\), \(A\) moves right at \(2\text{ m s}^{-1}\). Then \(B\) collides with \(C\), after which \(C\) moves right at \(3\text{ m s}^{-1}\). Determine whether \(A\) and \(B\) collide again.

Take right as positive.

### Collision 1: \(A\) with \(B\)

Let \(v_B\) be \(B\)'s velocity immediately after this collision. Conservation of momentum gives

\(1(4)+2(3)=1(2)+2v_B\)

\(10=2+2v_B\)

\(v_B=4\text{ m s}^{-1}\)

The positive sign means that \(B\) moves right. It can therefore catch the slower \(C\).

### Collision 2: \(B\) with \(C\)

The velocity of \(B\) just before this collision is the \(4\text{ m s}^{-1}\) found above. Let its velocity immediately afterwards be \(w_B\). Then

\(2(4)+3(1)=2w_B+3(3)\)

\(11=2w_B+9\)

\(w_B=1\text{ m s}^{-1}\)

Particle \(A\) is still to the left of \(B\), moving right at \(2\text{ m s}^{-1}\), while \(B\) now moves right at \(1\text{ m s}^{-1}\). Since

\(2>1\)

the separation decreases at \(1\text{ m s}^{-1}\). Therefore \(A\) will catch \(B\), so there will be a second \(A\)-\(B\) collision.

No factor of \(g\) is needed: the calculation uses masses and velocities, not weights.

## Self-check

After a sequence of collisions, particle \(P\) is to the left of particle \(Q\). Taking right as positive, their velocities are \(-1\text{ m s}^{-1}\) and \(-3\text{ m s}^{-1}\), respectively. Will they collide?

### Answer

Yes. Both move left, but \(Q\) is to the right and has the greater leftward speed. Equivalently, \(-1>-3\), so \(v_P>v_Q\) and the separation decreases at \(2\text{ m s}^{-1}\).

## Common exam slips

- Trying to solve all collisions with one diagram or one momentum equation instead of treating the events in order.
- Reusing a symbol such as \(v_B\) for different stages, then substituting the wrong value into the next collision.
- Using a speed as though it were automatically positive after choosing a direction. Momentum needs signed velocity.
- Bringing \(g\) into \(mv\), or using weight instead of mass. Collision momentum calculations are normally \(g\)-free.
- Conserving momentum for one particle alone, or for a particle striking a fixed wall without accounting for the wall's external impulse.
- Comparing only speed magnitudes. Check which particle is left or right and whether the gap is closing.
- Assuming that two particles moving in the same direction cannot collide. The particle behind can catch the one in front.
- Assuming that two left-moving particles cannot collide. The right-hand particle can catch the left-hand particle if it moves left faster.
- Using \(B\)'s velocity before collision 1 as its velocity before collision 2 instead of carrying forward the result from collision 1.
- Introducing an extra impact law that is outside this syllabus scope instead of using conservation of momentum and the information supplied.
